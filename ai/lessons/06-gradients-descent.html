Part 2 · Calculus

Partials, gradients & gradient descent

A model's loss depends on all of its parameters at once: millions of knobs, one number out. The gradient packs every "how much does the loss change if I turn this knob?" into a single vector that points uphill. Step the other way and repeat. That loop is how essentially every neural network is trained.

By the end you can

  • Read a function of two variables from a heatmap with contour lines, and compute partial derivatives as slopes of slices.
  • Explain why the gradient is perpendicular to contours and points in the direction of steepest ascent, using $D_{\mathbf u}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$.
  • Run gradient descent and predict, from the learning rate and curvature, whether it converges, oscillates or diverges.
  • Use the second-order Taylor expansion and the Hessian to derive the stability bound $\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}$ and explain zig-zagging via the condition number.
  • Recognise saddle points and local minima, and say why they matter (and don't) for deep learning.

    1. Functions of several variables

    In lesson 05 every function took one number and returned one number. A loss function takes many numbers (the parameters) and returns one (how bad the model is): $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$. With $n = 2$ we can still draw it. Think of $f(x, y)$ as the height of a landscape above the point $(x, y)$.

    Two pictures of the same landscape will be used throughout this lesson:

    • a heatmap: colour each point by its height (darker = lower here);
    • contour lines (level sets): curves along which $f$ is constant, like the lines on a hiking map. Where contours bunch together, the ground is steep. Where they spread apart, it's flat.

    2. Partial derivatives: slice and differentiate

    How does $f$ change if you move only in $x$? Freeze $y$ at some value $y_0$. Then $t\mapsto f(t, y_0)$ is an ordinary one-variable function: a slice through the landscape. Its derivative is the partial derivative with respect to $x$:

    $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x_0, y_0) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h,\,y_0) - f(x_0, y_0)}{h}.$$

    In practice: treat every other variable as a constant and differentiate as in lesson 05. For $f(x,y) = x^2y + \sin y$: $\;\partial f/\partial x = 2xy$ (the $\sin y$ is a constant, so it vanishes) and $\partial f/\partial y = x^2 + \cos y$.

    Try it: drag the point around the landscape. The blue line is the slice where $y$ is frozen. Its profile is drawn top-right, and the slope of its tangent is $\partial f/\partial x$. The orange line freezes $x$ instead (bottom-right, slope $\partial f/\partial y$). Find a spot where both slopes are zero: the bottom of the bowl.

    3. The gradient: steepest ascent, perpendicular to contours

    Stack all the partials into a vector. That vector is the gradient:

    $$\nabla f(\mathbf x) = \begin{bmatrix} \partial f/\partial x_1 \\ \vdots \\ \partial f/\partial x_n\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The gradient is to $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ what the derivative is to a one-variable function. Here's why. The linear approximation from lesson 05 becomes

    $$f(\mathbf x + \mathbf d) \approx f(\mathbf x) + \nabla f(\mathbf x)\cdot\mathbf d.$$

    Nudge $x_1$ by $d_1$ and $f$ moves by about $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}d_1$. Nudge every coordinate and, to first order, the effects just add up. The sum of products is a dot product (lesson 01).

    Directional derivatives

    Walk from $\mathbf x$ in a unit direction $\mathbf u$. Set $\mathbf d = t\mathbf u$ above: the rate of change of $f$ along $\mathbf u$ is the directional derivative

    $$D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf x) = \nabla f(\mathbf x)\cdot\mathbf u = \|\nabla f\|\,\cos\theta,$$

    where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf u$ and the gradient. Everything now follows from $\cos\theta$:

    • $\theta = 0$: the largest possible rate, $\|\nabla f\|$. The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is that steepest slope.
    • $\theta = 180°$: steepest descent, along $-\nabla f$.
    • $\theta = 90°$: rate zero. You're walking along a contour. So the gradient is perpendicular to the contour lines.
    Try it: drag the big point to move, and drag the small purple handle around the circle to choose a direction $\mathbf u$. The right panel plots $D_{\mathbf u}f$ against the direction's angle: it's always a cosine wave. Its peak sits exactly at the gradient's angle with height $\|\nabla f\|$, and its zeros are the two directions along the contour. Tick "gradient field" to see the arrows cross every contour at right angles.
    Worked example

    $f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2$ at $(1, 1)$. Partials: $\partial f/\partial x = 2x = 2$, $\partial f/\partial y = 6y = 6$, so $\nabla f = (2, 6)$.

    Along $\mathbf u = (1,1)/\sqrt2$: $D_{\mathbf u}f = (2 + 6)/\sqrt2 \approx 5.657$.

    Steepest: $\|\nabla f\| = \sqrt{4 + 36} = \sqrt{40}\approx 6.325$, in direction $(2,6)/\sqrt{40}$, which is not toward the origin $(-1,-1)$. Downhill on an elongated bowl doesn't point at the bottom. Hold on to that; it's the zig-zag of section 6.

    Along the contour: $\mathbf u \propto (6, -2)$, the gradient rotated 90°. Check: $(2,6)\cdot(6,-2) = 0$.

    4. Gradient descent

    To minimise $f$, repeatedly step a little way downhill:

    $$\boxed{\;\mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathbf x_k - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf x_k)\;}$$

    The step size $\eta$ is the learning rate. Why does it work? By the linear approximation, $f(\mathbf x - \eta\nabla f) \approx f(\mathbf x) - \eta\|\nabla f\|^2$. That is a decrease, as long as $\eta$ is small enough for the linear approximation to be trustworthy. "Small enough" is the whole story of this section and the next two.

    Try it: click anywhere on the landscape to set a start, then press "play". The right panel shows $\log_{10} f$ per iteration. On the round bowl, lr = 1 jumps straight to the minimum, while lr = 1.9 overshoots back and forth and still converges. Above 2 it diverges. On the ravine, push lr past $2/\lambda_{\max} = 0.2$ and watch the steep direction blow up while the shallow one has barely moved. On Rosenbrock, the path drops into the curved valley fast and then crawls along it for thousands of steps.
    Worked example: one dimension, three learning rates

    $f(x) = 2x^2$, so $f'(x) = 4x$ and the update is $x_{k+1} = x_k - 4\eta x_k = (1 - 4\eta)\,x_k$. Start at $x_0 = 1$.

    $\eta = 0.1$: factor $0.6$. $x$: $1, 0.6, 0.36, 0.216,\dots$ Smooth convergence.

    $\eta = 0.4$: factor $-0.6$. $x$: $1, -0.6, 0.36, -0.216,\dots$ Oscillates across the minimum but converges.

    $\eta = 0.6$: factor $-1.4$. $x$: $1, -1.4, 1.96, -2.744,\dots$ Diverges.

    The dividing line is $|1 - 4\eta| \lt 1 \iff \eta \lt 0.5 = 2/f''$. The curvature $f'' = 4$ sets the speed limit.

    In deep learning the gradient is averaged over a random mini-batch of data instead of the whole dataset. That's stochastic gradient descent (lesson 13 onward), and every optimiser you'll meet (momentum, RMSProp, Adam in lesson 18) is a refinement of this one-line update. The gradient itself is computed by backpropagation, which is the chain rule applied to vectors and matrices: lesson 07, then lesson 17.

    5. Curvature: second-order Taylor and the Hessian

    To understand when a step is too big we need the next term of the approximation. In one variable, $f(x+d) \approx f(x) + f'(x)d + \tfrac12 f''(x)d^2$. In many variables the second derivatives form a matrix, the Hessian:

    $$H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}, \qquad f(\mathbf x+\mathbf d) \approx f(\mathbf x) + \nabla f\cdot\mathbf d + \tfrac12\,\mathbf d^\top H\,\mathbf d.$$

    For smooth $f$ the mixed partials agree ($\partial^2 f/\partial x\partial y = \partial^2 f/\partial y\partial x$), so $H$ is symmetric. Lesson 04's spectral theorem then applies: $H$ has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. Each eigenvector is a direction, and its eigenvalue is the curvature of $f$ along that direction:

    • all eigenvalues $> 0$: locally a bowl (convex);
    • all $\lt 0$: a dome;
    • mixed signs: a saddle, curving up in some directions and down in others.
    Try it: drag the point. The solid grey curves are contours of the true $f(x,y) = \tfrac14x^4 - x^2 + y^2 + \tfrac12xy$. The orange curves are contours of its second-order Taylor model at the point, and the arrows are the Hessian's eigenvectors (length ∝ |eigenvalue|, green if positive, pink if negative). Near the point the two sets of contours agree. Out at $|x| > 1$ the model is an ellipse (a bowl). Drag toward $x = 0$ and one eigenvalue turns negative: the model becomes a saddle, with hyperbolic contours.

    A quadratic is its own Taylor expansion, so for $f(\mathbf x) = \tfrac12\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf x - \mathbf b^\top\mathbf x$ (with $A$ symmetric), the Hessian is $A$ everywhere and the gradient is exactly $A\mathbf x - \mathbf b$ (problem 8 derives it). Every smooth function looks like such a quadratic near a minimum. That's why the next section studies quadratics in detail.

    6. Why learning rates diverge: $\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}$, and the zig-zag

    Take the quadratic above with $A$ symmetric positive definite and minimiser $\mathbf x^* = A^{-1}\mathbf b$. Track the error $\mathbf e_k = \mathbf x_k - \mathbf x^*$. Since $\nabla f(\mathbf x_k) = A\mathbf x_k - \mathbf b = A\mathbf e_k$,

    $$\mathbf e_{k+1} = \mathbf e_k - \eta A\mathbf e_k = (I - \eta A)\,\mathbf e_k.$$

    Write $\mathbf e_k$ in $A$'s eigenvector basis (lesson 04). Along eigenvector $\mathbf q_i$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, the matrix $I - \eta A$ just multiplies by $1 - \eta\lambda_i$. So each eigen-direction is an independent 1-D problem, exactly the worked example of section 4:

    $$\text{component } i \text{ after } k \text{ steps} = (1 - \eta\lambda_i)^k\times\text{(start)}.$$

    Every component shrinks iff $|1 - \eta\lambda_i| \lt 1$ for all $i$, that is, $0 \lt \eta \lt 2/\lambda_i$. The tightest of these is set by the largest eigenvalue:

    $$\boxed{\;0 \lt \eta \lt \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}\;}$$

    The overall speed is set by the slowest factor $\max_i|1-\eta\lambda_i|$. Small $\eta$ makes the flat direction ($\lambda_{\min}$) crawl. Large $\eta$ makes the steep direction ($\lambda_{\max}$) bounce. The best compromise balances the two: $\eta^* = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max}}$, which gives the rate

    $$\max_i|1-\eta^*\lambda_i| = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} \text{ (the condition number).}$$
    Try it: left, 40 steps of gradient descent on $\tfrac12(x^2 + \kappa y^2)$, so $\lambda_{\min} = 1$, $\lambda_{\max} = \kappa$. Right, the per-step shrink factor of each eigen-direction against the learning rate (as a fraction of $2/\lambda_{\max}$). The green line is the flat $x$-direction and the pink one the steep $y$-direction. The thick envelope is the overall rate. Raise $\kappa$: the envelope's lowest point creeps toward 1, and the path zig-zags across the ravine while inching along it. Push lr past 1.0 and the steep direction's factor exceeds 1: divergence.

    Two facts to remember. The steepest curvature sets the maximum learning rate. The ratio of steepest to flattest curvature sets how slow convergence is. With $\kappa = 100$ the best rate is $99/101\approx0.98$: about 680 steps per factor of $10^{6}$. Ill-conditioning, not lack of steps, is what makes plain gradient descent slow.

    This is the theory behind a lot of deep-learning practice. Standardising input features (lesson 13) and normalisation layers (lesson 18) make the loss surface rounder, which lowers $\kappa$. Momentum and Adam (lesson 18) are ways to get around a large $\kappa$ without computing the Hessian. Learning-rate warm-up exists partly because early in training $\lambda_{\max}$ can be large.

    7. Critical points: minima, maxima, saddles

    Gradient descent stops wherever $\nabla f = \mathbf 0$, a critical point. The Hessian there tells you what kind of point it is (the second-derivative test): all eigenvalues positive means a local minimum, all negative a local maximum, and mixed signs a saddle. If some eigenvalue is zero the test is inconclusive.

    Two things can go wrong:

    • Local minima: a valley that isn't the deepest one. Gradient descent only sees local slope, so which valley you end in depends on where you start.
    • Saddle points: the gradient is zero but there's a way down. Exactly on the ridge, gradient descent stalls; a hair off it, the unstable direction grows like $(1 + \eta|\lambda|)^k$ and you eventually escape. But "eventually" can be slow, because the gradient is tiny near the saddle.
    Try it: click to drop a ball, which then runs gradient descent (lr 0.05). On the saddle landscape $\tfrac12x^2 + \tfrac14y^4 - \tfrac12y^2$, drop a ball exactly on the $x$-axis ($y = 0$; tick "snap to y = 0"): it rolls to the saddle at the origin and stops. Drop it a hair off the axis and it lingers near the saddle, then escapes to a minimum at $y=\pm1$. On two valleys, press "drop 40 balls": each ball's colour shows which minimum it reached. The left valley is the global minimum, but many balls settle in the right one.

    In 2-D, local minima look like the main danger. In the millions of dimensions of a neural network, a random critical point needs every eigenvalue to be positive to be a minimum, which is very unlikely. Most critical points are saddles, and the noise of stochastic gradients pushes you off them. Empirically, the minima that large networks find tend to be about equally good. The practical difficulties are more often plateaus and bad conditioning than "getting stuck in a bad local minimum".

    8. Problems

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    Let $f(x,y) = x^2y + 3y^2$. Compute $\nabla f(1, 2)$. Enter the two components.

    Solution

    $\partial f/\partial x = 2xy = 4$. $\partial f/\partial y = x^2 + 6y = 1 + 12 = 13$. So $\nabla f(1,2) = (4, 13)$.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    For the same $f$ at $(1,2)$, find the directional derivative toward $(3, 4)$ (i.e. along the unit vector in that direction), and the largest directional derivative over all directions.

    Hint

    Normalise first: $\mathbf u = (3,4)/5$. The largest value is $\|\nabla f\|$. Enter both numbers separated by a comma.

    Solution

    $D_{\mathbf u}f = (4, 13)\cdot(0.6, 0.8) = 2.4 + 10.4 = 12.8$. The maximum is $\|\nabla f\| = \sqrt{16 + 169} = \sqrt{185}\approx13.60$, along $\nabla f$ itself. $(3,4)$ is a pretty good direction ($\cos\theta = 12.8/13.6 \approx 0.94$) but not the best.

    Problem 3
    warm-up

    Take one gradient descent step on $f(x,y) = x^2 + 3y^2$ from $(2, 1)$ with $\eta = 0.1$. Enter the new point. By how much did $f$ drop?

    Solution

    $\nabla f(2,1) = (4, 6)$. New point $(2,1) - 0.1(4,6) = (1.6, 0.4)$. $f$ goes from $4 + 3 = 7$ to $2.56 + 0.48 = 3.04$, a drop of $3.96$. The linear prediction was $\eta\|\nabla f\|^2 = 0.1\cdot52 = 5.2$. The real drop is smaller because the curvature term $\tfrac12\mathbf d^\top H\mathbf d$ eats into it.

    Problem 4
    core · proof

    Prove that the gradient is perpendicular to the level set through a point. Let $\mathbf r(t)$ be any smooth curve lying in the level set $\{f = c\}$ with $\mathbf r(0) = \mathbf x$.

    Hint

    $f(\mathbf r(t)) = c$ for all $t$. Differentiate both sides at $t=0$ using the multivariable chain rule $\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t)) = \nabla f(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)$.

    Solution

    Differentiating $f(\mathbf r(t)) = c$ gives $\nabla f(\mathbf x)\cdot\mathbf r'(0) = 0$. $\mathbf r'(0)$ is the velocity of a curve in the level set, i.e. a tangent direction to the contour. Since the curve was arbitrary, $\nabla f$ is orthogonal to every tangent direction of the level set. (The chain rule used here is $D_{\mathbf u}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$ applied along the curve. Lesson 07 generalises it to Jacobians.)

    Problem 5
    core

    Gradient descent on $f(x,y) = \tfrac12(x^2 + 10y^2)$. (a) What is the largest stable learning rate? (b) What is the optimal learning rate, and the resulting per-step error factor? (c) About how many steps does it take to shrink the error by a factor of $10^6$? Enter (a) and the factor from (b).

    Solution

    The Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(1, 10)$, so $\lambda_{\min} = 1$, $\lambda_{\max} = 10$, $\kappa = 10$. (a) $2/\lambda_{\max} = 0.2$. (b) $\eta^* = 2/(1+10) \approx 0.1818$, factor $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) = 9/11 \approx 0.818$. (c) $\ln(10^{-6})/\ln(9/11) \approx 68.8$, so 69 steps. At $\eta^*$ both directions shrink by the same $9/11$ per step, the $y$ one with alternating sign.

    Problem 6
    core

    Find and classify all critical points of $f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$.

    Solution

    $\nabla f = (3x^2 - 3,\; 2y) = \mathbf 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm1,\ y = 0$. Hessian $H = \operatorname{diag}(6x, 2)$. At $(1,0)$: $\operatorname{diag}(6, 2)$, both positive: local minimum. At $(-1, 0)$: $\operatorname{diag}(-6, 2)$, mixed signs: saddle. There's no global minimum at all, since $f\to-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$. A local minimum can be the only minimum and still not be the lowest point.

    Problem 7
    core

    The Rosenbrock function $f = (1-x)^2 + 100(y - x^2)^2$ has its minimum at $(1,1)$, where the Hessian is $\begin{bmatrix}802 & -400\\-400 & 200\end{bmatrix}$. Verify the Hessian, then find (approximately) its condition number and the largest stable learning rate near the minimum.

    Hint

    For a symmetric 2×2 matrix $\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{bmatrix}$ the eigenvalues are $\tfrac{a+d}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{a-d}{2}\right)^2 + b^2}$. Enter $\kappa$ and the learning-rate bound.

    Solution

    $f_x = -2(1-x) - 400x(y - x^2)$, $f_y = 200(y-x^2)$. Then $f_{xx} = 2 - 400y + 1200x^2 = 802$, $f_{xy} = -400x = -400$, $f_{yy} = 200$ at $(1,1)$. Eigenvalues: $501 \pm \sqrt{301^2 + 400^2} = 501\pm500.6$, so $\lambda\approx 1001.6$ and $0.3994$. $\kappa\approx2508$, and the bound is $2/1001.6\approx0.0020$. That's why the figure in section 4 needs a tiny learning rate and thousands of steps for Rosenbrock.

    Problem 8
    core · derivation

    Let $A$ be a symmetric $n\times n$ matrix and $f(\mathbf x) = \tfrac12\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf x - \mathbf b^\top\mathbf x$. Show that $\nabla f = A\mathbf x - \mathbf b$ and that the Hessian is $A$. What changes if $A$ is not symmetric?

    Hint

    Write $\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf x = \sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_ix_j$ and take $\partial/\partial x_k$. The variable $x_k$ appears when $i=k$ and when $j=k$.

    Solution

    $\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\sum_{ij}A_{ij}x_ix_j = \sum_j A_{kj}x_j + \sum_i A_{ik}x_i = (A\mathbf x)_k + (A^\top\mathbf x)_k$. So $\nabla(\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf x) = (A + A^\top)\mathbf x$, which is $2A\mathbf x$ for symmetric $A$. With the $\tfrac12$ and the linear term, $\nabla f = A\mathbf x - \mathbf b$. Differentiating once more, $\partial(A\mathbf x - \mathbf b)_k/\partial x_l = A_{kl}$, so $H = A$. For non-symmetric $A$, only the symmetric part $\tfrac12(A + A^\top)$ matters: $\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf x$ can't see the antisymmetric part. Lesson 07 redoes this with matrix-calculus rules.

    Problem 9
    stretch

    On a quadratic $f = \tfrac12\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf x$, instead of a fixed $\eta$ choose the step that minimises $f$ along the negative gradient ("exact line search"). Show that $\eta^* = \dfrac{\mathbf g^\top\mathbf g}{\mathbf g^\top A\mathbf g}$ where $\mathbf g = A\mathbf x$. Evaluate it for $A = \operatorname{diag}(1, 10)$ at $\mathbf x = (1, 1)$.

    Solution

    $\phi(\eta) = f(\mathbf x - \eta\mathbf g) = f(\mathbf x) - \eta\,\mathbf g^\top\mathbf g + \tfrac12\eta^2\,\mathbf g^\top A\mathbf g$ (second-order Taylor, exact for a quadratic). Then $\phi'(\eta) = 0$ gives $\eta^* = \mathbf g^\top\mathbf g / \mathbf g^\top A\mathbf g$. Here $\mathbf g = (1, 10)$, $\mathbf g^\top\mathbf g = 101$, $\mathbf g^\top A\mathbf g = 1 + 1000 = 1001$, so $\eta^* \approx 0.1009$. The denominator is the curvature along $\mathbf g$, and the gradient points mostly along the steep direction. So the "best" step is short, and the path still zig-zags: each line-search step ends exactly perpendicular to the previous one.

    9. Quick check

    At a point on a hiking map, the gradient of altitude points…
    Along a contour the altitude doesn't change, so $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u = 0$ there. The gradient points uphill (steepest ascent). It does not generally point at the summit: on an elongated hill, steepest isn't the same as straight-to-the-top.
    Gradient descent on a quadratic whose Hessian has eigenvalues 1 and 10, with $\eta = 0.25$. What happens?
    The bound is $\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max} = 0.2$. The flat direction's factor $|1 - 0.25| = 0.75$ is fine, but the steep one is multiplied by $-1.5$ every step and explodes.
    Your loss curve shows rapid initial progress, then the loss decreases painfully slowly while the parameters oscillate back and forth. The most likely cause is…
    Bouncing across the steep direction while crawling along the flat one is the signature of a large condition number. (a) would diverge; (b) wouldn't keep decreasing. Fixes: feature scaling, normalisation, momentum, Adam (lessons 13, 18).
    At a critical point the Hessian has eigenvalues $3$, $0.5$ and $-0.01$. This point is…
    One negative eigenvalue is enough: moving along its eigenvector decreases $f$. Its smallness means the escape is slow. That's typical of saddles in high-dimensional loss surfaces.

    10. Summary

    ConceptFormulaMeaning
    Partial derivative$\partial f/\partial x_i$slope of the slice along axis $i$
    Gradient$\nabla f = (\partial f/\partial x_1,\dots)$steepest-ascent direction; $\perp$ contours
    Directional derivative$D_{\mathbf u}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$slope when walking along $\mathbf u$
    Gradient descent$\mathbf x\leftarrow\mathbf x - \eta\nabla f$step downhill, repeat
    Hessian$H_{ij} = \partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j$curvature; eigenvalues classify critical points
    Stability (quadratic)$\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}$steepest curvature caps the learning rate
    Convergence rate$(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ill-conditioning ⇒ slow zig-zag
