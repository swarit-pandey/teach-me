Part 5 · Deep learning

Optimizers, initialization & normalization

Backprop (lesson 17) tells you which way is downhill. It doesn't tell you how big a step to take, where to start, or how to stop signals from dying or blowing up on their way through 100 layers. This lesson covers the engineering that turns "gradient descent works in principle" into "this transformer actually trains": Adam, learning-rate schedules, careful initialization, LayerNorm and residual connections. Every modern LLM uses all five.

By the end you can

  • Explain why plain SGD struggles in ravines, and how momentum, RMSProp and Adam fix different parts of the problem.
  • Derive Adam's bias correction and the effective step size of momentum.
  • Design a warmup + cosine learning-rate schedule and say why each half exists.
  • Derive Xavier and He initialization from variance propagation (lesson 09).
  • Explain vanishing/exploding gradients as a product of Jacobians, and how LayerNorm and residual connections defeat it.

    1. The trouble with plain gradient descent

    Gradient descent, $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla L(\theta)$, uses one learning rate for every direction. Lesson 06 showed what goes wrong on an elongated bowl ("ravine") like $L = \tfrac12(x^2 + 50y^2)$. The gradient points mostly across the ravine, not along it. Stability in the steep direction requires $\eta \lt 2/50$. At that step size, progress in the shallow direction shrinks by a factor of only $1 - \eta \approx 0.96$ per step. The number of steps scales with the condition number, the ratio of the largest to smallest curvature (here 50). Real networks have condition numbers in the millions.

    Two more problems show up in practice:

    • Noise. We never compute the true gradient. We estimate it from a minibatch of $B$ examples. The estimate is unbiased, but its standard deviation shrinks only like $1/\sqrt{B}$ (lesson 09's CLT).
    • Wildly different scales. Different parameters (an embedding row for a rare token versus a LayerNorm gain) see gradients that differ by orders of magnitude. One global $\eta$ is too big for some and too small for others.

    2. Momentum: a heavy ball

    Instead of stepping along the current gradient, keep a running velocity and step along that:

    $$\mathbf{v}_t = \beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + \mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta\,\mathbf{v}_t, \qquad \beta \approx 0.9.$$

    Unroll it: $\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{g}_t + \beta\mathbf{g}_{t-1} + \beta^2\mathbf{g}_{t-2} + \cdots$, an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients. Two effects follow:

    • Along the ravine the gradients keep pointing the same way, so they add up. With a constant gradient $\mathbf{g}$, $\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{g}(1 + \beta + \beta^2 + \cdots) = \mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$. The effective step is $\eta/(1-\beta)$, which is 10× larger for $\beta = 0.9$.
    • Across the ravine the gradient flips sign every step, so consecutive terms cancel and the zig-zag is damped.

    Physically: a ball with mass rolling in the loss landscape. It builds up speed on consistent slopes and averages out the jitter.

    3. RMSProp and Adam: a step size per parameter

    RMSProp attacks the scale problem. Keep a running average of the squared gradient, per coordinate, and divide by its square root:

    $$\mathbf{s}_t = \beta_2\mathbf{s}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\,\mathbf{g}_t^2, \qquad \theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta\,\frac{\mathbf{g}_t}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_t} + \epsilon}.$$

    (Squares, roots and division are element-wise.) A coordinate whose gradients are typically huge gets a small step, and one with tiny gradients gets a big step. The update has roughly unit size in every direction, so its magnitude is set by $\eta$ alone. In the ravine, the steep and shallow directions are equalized. Problem 9 shows the update is even invariant to rescaling the loss.

    Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2014) combines both ideas: momentum for direction, RMSProp for scale.

    $$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{m}_t &= \beta_1\mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_1)\,\mathbf{g}_t & \text{(1st moment: mean)}\\ \mathbf{v}_t &= \beta_2\mathbf{v}_{t-1} + (1-\beta_2)\,\mathbf{g}_t^2 & \text{(2nd moment: uncentred variance)}\\ \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t &= \mathbf{m}_t/(1-\beta_1^t), \quad \hat{\mathbf{v}}_t = \mathbf{v}_t/(1-\beta_2^t) & \text{(bias correction)}\\ \theta_t &= \theta_{t-1} - \eta\,\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}_t} + \epsilon) \end{aligned}$$

    Defaults: $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$. Typical $\eta$ is $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$ for transformers.

    Deriving the bias correction

    $\mathbf{m}$ starts at $\mathbf{0}$, so early on it is dragged toward zero. Unroll: $\mathbf{m}_t = (1-\beta_1)\sum_{k=1}^{t}\beta_1^{t-k}\mathbf{g}_k$. If the gradients are drawn from a distribution with mean $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}]$, then by linearity of expectation (lesson 09)

    $$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_t] = (1-\beta_1)\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}]\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\beta_1^k = (1-\beta_1)\frac{1-\beta_1^t}{1-\beta_1}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}] = (1-\beta_1^t)\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}].$$

    So dividing by $1-\beta_1^t$ makes $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t$ unbiased, and the same argument works for $\mathbf{v}$. The correction matters most for $\mathbf{v}$: with $\beta_2 = 0.999$, the uncorrected $\mathbf{v}$ is about 1000× too small at step 1, which would make the first steps huge.

    Worked example: Adam's first steps

    One parameter, $\eta = 0.01$, gradient $g_1 = 2$. Then $m_1 = 0.1\cdot2 = 0.2$ and $v_1 = 0.001\cdot4 = 0.004$. Corrected: $\hat m_1 = 0.2/0.1 = 2$, $\hat v_1 = 0.004/0.001 = 4$. Step: $0.01\cdot 2/\sqrt4 = 0.01$. The first Adam step has size exactly $\eta$, whatever the gradient's magnitude: it's $\eta\cdot\mathrm{sign}(g)$.

    Without correction: $0.01\cdot0.2/\sqrt{0.004} = 0.0316$, about $3.16\eta$, and with $\beta_2=0.999$ the inflation persists for hundreds of steps.

    Second step with $g_2 = -2$: $m_2 = 0.9\cdot0.2 - 0.2 = -0.02$, $\hat m_2 = -0.02/0.19 = -0.105$, $v_2 = 0.999\cdot0.004 + 0.004 = 0.007996$, $\hat v_2 = 0.007996/0.001999 = 4$. Step $= 0.01\cdot0.105/2 \approx 0.0005$. The sign flip is mostly cancelled by momentum.

    Try it: press race. SGD, momentum, RMSProp and Adam start from the same point. Click anywhere to restart from there. On the ravine, raise the SGD learning rate past 0.04 and watch it diverge across the steep direction, while the adaptive methods take near-equal steps in both directions. Momentum with $\beta = 0.9$ overshoots wildly across the ravine (it is badly under-damped there); lower $\beta$ to about 0.5 and it calms down. On Rosenbrock (a curved banana-shaped valley with its minimum at (1, 1)), SGD reaches the valley floor and then crawls. Momentum and Adam roll along it. RMSProp with a fixed rate keeps rattling near the end: that's why we decay the learning rate (§5).

    In practice: AdamW (Adam with "decoupled" weight decay: $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\lambda\theta$ applied directly instead of adding $\lambda\theta$ to the gradient) is the default for transformers, including GPT-style models in lessons 24–25. Its memory cost is two extra numbers ($m$, $v$) per parameter. That's why training a 7B model needs far more GPU memory than running it.

    4. Minibatch noise

    The full-data loss is an average, $L(\theta) = \frac1N\sum_i \ell_i(\theta)$. A minibatch gradient $\hat{\mathbf{g}} = \frac1B\sum_{i\in\text{batch}}\nabla\ell_i$ is an unbiased estimate of $\nabla L$. If the per-example gradients have covariance $\Sigma$, the estimate has covariance $\Sigma/B$ (lesson 09). So SGD is gradient descent plus noise whose size is $\propto \eta/\sqrt{B}$. Near the minimum the true gradient vanishes but the noise doesn't. With a constant learning rate, SGD ends up jittering around the minimum in a cloud whose size scales with $\eta$.

    Try it: fitting $y = wx + b$ to 200 noisy points. The contours are the full-data MSE in $(w, b)$ and the star is the least-squares optimum (lesson 13). Press run. With batch size 200 (full batch) the path is smooth. Drop to $B = 1$ and the path becomes a random walk that never settles. Tick decay lr (the learning rate falls like $1/(1 + t/50)$) and the cloud shrinks onto the optimum. Noise isn't all bad: it helps escape sharp, poorly-generalizing minima.

    5. Learning-rate schedules: warmup + cosine

    Nearly every large model is trained with the same shape of schedule:

    $$\eta_t = \begin{cases} \eta_{\max}\cdot\dfrac{t+1}{T_w} & t \lt T_w \quad\text{(linear warmup)}\\[2mm] \eta_{\min} + \tfrac12(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})\big(1 + \cos(\pi\,\tfrac{t - T_w}{T - T_w})\big) & t \ge T_w \quad\text{(cosine decay)}\end{cases}$$
    • Why decay? From §4: the noise floor scales with $\eta$. Big steps early make fast progress, and small steps late settle into the minimum. Cosine is just a smooth, hyperparameter-free way to go from big to small.
    • Why warmup? At step 0 the weights are random, gradients can be large and erratic, and Adam's $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ is estimated from only a few samples. Taking full-size steps immediately can push the network into a bad region it never recovers from. This is especially true for transformers, where early instability in attention and LayerNorm is common. A few hundred to a few thousand steps of ramp-up avoids it.
    Try it: adjust the warmup fraction and the floor $\eta_{\min}/\eta_{\max}$, and drag the step slider to read off $\eta_t$. Faint curves for comparison: a constant rate and the older "step decay" (÷10 at fixed milestones). The cosine spends a long time near $\eta_{\max}$, then falls fastest in the middle and flattens out at the end.

    6. Initialization: keeping the signal alive

    Lesson 16 said "initialize randomly". The scale matters enormously. Consider one unit in a layer with $n$ inputs: $z = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i$. Assume the $w_i$ and $x_i$ are independent, $\mathbb{E}[w_i] = 0$, and $\mathrm{Var}(w_i) = \sigma_w^2$. Then by lesson 09 (variances of independent terms add, and $\mathrm{Var}(wx) = \mathbb{E}[w^2]\mathbb{E}[x^2]$ when $\mathbb{E}[w]=0$):

    $$\mathrm{Var}(z) = n\,\sigma_w^2\,\mathbb{E}[x^2].$$

    Each layer multiplies the signal's second moment by $n\sigma_w^2$, or by $n\sigma_w^2/2$ after a ReLU (below). After $L$ layers that factor is raised to the $L$-th power. If it's 0.5, the signal shrinks by $2^{-L}$. If it's 2, it grows by $2^L$. Deep networks need the factor to be exactly 1.

    • Linear or tanh (near 0): $\mathbb{E}[x^2] \approx \mathrm{Var}(z_\text{prev})$, so we need $n\sigma_w^2 = 1$: $\sigma_w^2 = 1/n_\text{in}$. The backward pass (lesson 17: $\bar X = \bar Z W^\top$) multiplies gradient variance by $n_\text{out}\sigma_w^2$, so it wants $1/n_\text{out}$. Xavier/Glorot init compromises: $\sigma_w^2 = \dfrac{2}{n_\text{in} + n_\text{out}}$.
    • ReLU: $z$ is symmetric about 0, so $\mathrm{relu}(z)$ keeps exactly half of $z$'s second moment: $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{relu}(z)^2] = \tfrac12\mathrm{Var}(z)$. To compensate, double the variance. That's He/Kaiming init: $\sigma_w^2 = \dfrac{2}{n_\text{in}}$.
    Worked example: He init for a 512-wide ReLU layer

    $n_\text{in} = 512$: $\sigma_w = \sqrt{2/512} = 0.0625$. Suppose the incoming activations have $\mathbb{E}[x^2] = 1$. Then $\mathrm{Var}(z) = 512\cdot0.0625^2\cdot1 = 2$, and after ReLU, $\mathbb{E}[x_\text{next}^2] = 2/2 = 1$. The second moment is preserved, layer after layer.

    With "standard normal" weights ($\sigma_w = 1$) instead, the factor per layer is $512/2 = 256$. After 10 layers that's $256^{10} \approx 10^{24}$, which overflows float16 in about 4 layers.

    Try it: a 10-layer, 128-wide network runs live in your browser on a batch of Gaussian inputs. Top: a histogram of each layer's activations. Bottom: $\log_{10}$ of the activation std (blue) and of the backward gradient std (pink) per layer. Try std 0.01 with tanh (everything collapses to 0), std 1.0 with tanh (everything saturates at ±1, so gradients die), and Xavier vs He with ReLU (only He stays flat). Then tick LayerNorm: even a terrible init is rescued, because every layer re-standardizes its input.

    7. Vanishing and exploding gradients

    The backward pass makes the same point from the other side. With $\mathbf{h}_\ell = \phi(\mathbf{h}_{\ell-1}W_\ell)$, the chain rule (lesson 07) gives

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_0} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_L}\;J_L J_{L-1}\cdots J_1, \qquad J_\ell = \frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_\ell}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{\ell-1}} = \mathrm{diag}(\phi'(\mathbf{z}_\ell))\,W_\ell^\top.$$

    A product of $L$ matrices behaves like a product of $L$ numbers. If the typical stretch factor (roughly the largest singular value, lesson 04) is below 1, the gradient decays exponentially with depth: it vanishes. Above 1, it explodes. Saturated tanh/sigmoid units make it worse, because $\phi'\approx0$ in the flat tails. The early layers then receive essentially no learning signal. Good initialization only puts the factor near 1 at the start. Training can push it away again. Two architectural fixes keep it there.

    8. Normalization: BatchNorm vs LayerNorm

    Rather than hoping activations stay well-scaled, force them to. Standardize (subtract mean, divide by std), then let the network re-scale with learned parameters $\gamma, \beta$:

    $$\hat x = \frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y = \gamma\odot\hat x + \beta.$$

    The only question is which numbers $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are computed over. Take an activation matrix of shape (batch $N$) × (features $D$):

LayerNorm doesn't care about $N$.
    Worked example: LayerNorm on one token

    $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2, 3, 6)$, $\gamma = \mathbf{1}$, $\beta = \mathbf{0}$. Mean $\mu = 12/4 = 3$. Variance $\sigma^2 = \tfrac14(4 + 1 + 0 + 9) = 3.5$, so $\sigma = 1.871$. Output: $(1-3, 2-3, 3-3, 6-3)/1.871 = (-1.069, -0.535, 0, 1.604)$. It has mean 0 and variance 1 whatever the scale of $\mathbf{x}$: multiply $\mathbf{x}$ by 1000 and the output is unchanged.

    Its backward pass is a nice exercise in lesson 07's matrix calculus (problem 6). With $\mathbf{g} = \gamma\odot\partial L/\partial\mathbf{y}$, it comes out as

    $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{\sigma}\Big(\mathbf{g} - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{g}) - \hat{\mathbf{x}}\,\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{g}\odot\hat{\mathbf{x}})\Big).$$

    Read it geometrically. The gradient is $\mathbf{g}$ with its component along $\mathbf{1}$ removed (shifting $\mathbf{x}$ doesn't change $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$) and its component along $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ removed (scaling $\mathbf{x}$ doesn't either). That's two projections, straight out of lesson 01.

    9. Residual connections: gradient highways

    A residual (skip) connection computes $\mathbf{h}_\ell = \mathbf{h}_{\ell-1} + F_\ell(\mathbf{h}_{\ell-1})$ instead of $\mathbf{h}_\ell = F_\ell(\mathbf{h}_{\ell-1})$. The layer learns a correction to its input rather than a whole new representation. Its Jacobian is

    $$\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_\ell}{\partial\mathbf{h}_{\ell-1}} = I + J_{F_\ell} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_0} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_L}\prod_{\ell}(I + J_{F_\ell}).$$

    Expand the product: it contains the term $I$ (the gradient flows straight from the loss to the input untouched), plus all the single $J$ terms, pairs, and so on. Even if every $J_F$ is small, the identity path delivers the gradient intact. In lesson 17's language, the + node distributes the gradient: one copy goes through $F$ and one bypasses it. This is why ResNets could reach 152 layers in 2015, and why every transformer block (lesson 23) is written $\mathbf{x} + \mathrm{Attention}(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{x}))$, then $\mathbf{x} + \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{x}))$.

    Try it: gradient size at each layer of a deep tanh network, measured by backprop in your browser, relative to the gradient at the output (log scale). With weight scale 0.5, the plain network's gradient (pink) decays exponentially toward the input. The residual network (blue) never drops below the output's gradient. Push the scale to 1.5: the plain net now hovers (tanh saturation keeps it bounded), while the residual sum grows. Keeping residual streams in check is one more job for LayerNorm.

    The modern recipe, which you'll use for the GPT in lesson 24: residual connections around every sub-block, LayerNorm before each sub-block ("pre-norm"), small or scaled init on the layers that write into the residual stream, AdamW, and linear warmup into cosine decay.

    10. Problems

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    With momentum $\beta = 0.9$ and a constant gradient, by what factor is the terminal step larger than plain SGD's step at the same $\eta$? What about $\beta = 0.99$? Enter both.

    Solution

    $\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{g}/(1-\beta)$: factor $1/(1-\beta)$, which is 10 and 100. This is why you usually lower $\eta$ when you raise $\beta$.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    Adam with default $\beta$s sees $g_1 = 1$, then $g_2 = -1$. Compute $\hat m_2$.

    Solution

    $m_1 = 0.1$, $m_2 = 0.09 - 0.1 = -0.01$, $\hat m_2 = -0.01/(1 - 0.81) = -0.0526$. And $v_2 = 0.000999 + 0.001 = 0.001999$, $\hat v_2 = 0.001999/0.001999 = 1$, so the step is $+0.0526\,\eta$. The reversal is heavily damped.

    Problem 3
    warm-up

    He initialization for a ReLU layer with $n_\text{in} = 1024$: what is the weight standard deviation?

    Solution

    $\sqrt{2/1024} = 0.0442$.

    Problem 4
    core

    A 10-layer linear network of width 100 is initialized with standard-normal weights ($\sigma_w = 1$). By what factor is the output's variance larger than the input's? Enter $\log_{10}$ of the factor.

    Solution

    Each layer multiplies the variance by $n\sigma_w^2 = 100$. Ten layers give $100^{10} = 10^{20}$. Xavier ($\sigma_w^2 = 1/100$) makes the factor exactly 1.

    Problem 5
    core

    A run uses $T = 1000$ steps, $T_w = 100$ warmup steps, $\eta_{\max} = 3\times10^{-4}$ and $\eta_{\min} = 3\times10^{-5}$. What is $\eta$ at step 550?

    Solution

    Progress $= (550-100)/900 = 0.5$, so $\cos(\pi/2) = 0$ and $\eta = \eta_{\min} + \tfrac12(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min}) = 3\times10^{-5} + 1.35\times10^{-4} = 1.65\times10^{-4}$, the midpoint.

    Problem 6
    core · derivation

    Derive the LayerNorm backward formula in §8. Work with one row $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^D$, $\gamma = \mathbf{1}$, $\beta = \mathbf{0}$, $\sigma = \sqrt{\mathrm{var}+\epsilon}$, and upstream gradient $\mathbf{g} = \partial L/\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}$.

    Hint

    $\hat x_j = (x_j - \mu)/\sigma$ depends on $x_i$ directly (if $i=j$), through $\mu$ ($\partial\mu/\partial x_i = 1/D$), and through $\sigma$ ($\partial\sigma/\partial x_i = (x_i - \mu)/(D\sigma) = \hat x_i/D$).

    Solution

    $\dfrac{\partial\hat x_j}{\partial x_i} = \dfrac{\delta_{ij} - 1/D}{\sigma} - \dfrac{(x_j-\mu)}{\sigma^2}\cdot\dfrac{\hat x_i}{D} = \dfrac1\sigma\Big(\delta_{ij} - \dfrac1D - \dfrac{\hat x_j\hat x_i}{D}\Big)$.

    Then $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial x_i} = \sum_j g_j\dfrac{\partial\hat x_j}{\partial x_i} = \dfrac1\sigma\Big(g_i - \dfrac1D\sum_j g_j - \hat x_i\,\dfrac1D\sum_j g_j\hat x_j\Big)$, which is the formula. As a matrix, the Jacobian is $\frac1\sigma(I - \frac1D\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top - \frac1D\hat{\mathbf{x}}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^\top)$. Since $\hat{\mathbf{x}}\perp\mathbf{1}$ and $\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|^2 = D$ (up to $\epsilon$), this is $\frac1\sigma$ times a projection matrix (lesson 01).

    Problem 7
    core

    Why do transformers use LayerNorm rather than BatchNorm? Give two concrete reasons.

    Solution

    (1) Generation runs with a batch of one sequence, one token at a time. BatchNorm's batch statistics are meaningless there and differ from training. (2) Sequences have variable length and padding, so "the batch" of positions mixes real tokens with padding and leaks information across examples. Also, (3) BatchNorm makes one example's output depend on the others in its batch, which complicates causal masking and distributed training. LayerNorm normalizes each token's vector alone and behaves identically at training and inference.

    Problem 8
    stretch

    (a) Show that $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{relu}(z)^2] = \tfrac12\mathrm{Var}(z)$ for any zero-mean distribution symmetric about 0. (b) A residual network has $L$ blocks, each with Jacobian $I + J_\ell$ where $\|J_\ell\| \le \delta$. Bound the smallest possible gain $\|\prod_\ell(I + J_\ell)\mathbf{u}\|$ for a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$, and compare with a plain network where each $\|J_\ell\|\le\delta \lt 1$.

    Solution

    (a) $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{relu}(z)^2] = \mathbb{E}[z^2\mathbb{1}[z>0]]$. By symmetry, $z^2\mathbb{1}[z>0]$ and $z^2\mathbb{1}[z \lt 0]$ have equal expectations, and they sum to $z^2$ (except at $z=0$, which contributes nothing), so each is $\frac12\mathbb{E}[z^2] = \frac12\mathrm{Var}(z)$.

    (b) For each factor, $\|(I+J)\mathbf{v}\| \ge \|\mathbf{v}\| - \|J\mathbf{v}\| \ge (1-\delta)\|\mathbf{v}\|$, so the gain is at least $(1-\delta)^L \approx e^{-L\delta}$. If each residual branch is small, $\delta \lesssim 1/L$, the gradient keeps at least about $e^{-1}$ of its size however deep the network is. For the plain network the gain is at most $\delta^L$: with $\delta = 0.5$ and $L = 30$, that's $10^{-9}$. This is why residual branches are initialized small (GPT-2 scales them by $1/\sqrt{L}$).

    Problem 9
    core

    Show that if you multiply the loss by a constant $c > 0$, plain SGD's trajectory changes, but Adam's doesn't (ignoring $\epsilon$).

    Solution

    Every gradient becomes $c\,\mathbf{g}$. SGD's step becomes $c\eta\mathbf{g}$, so it's effectively a different learning rate. For Adam, $\mathbf{m}\to c\mathbf{m}$ and $\mathbf{v}\to c^2\mathbf{v}$, so $\hat{\mathbf{m}}/\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}}$ is unchanged. That's why Adam learning rates transfer across losses and architectures far better than SGD's. The workbook tests this property for RMSProp.

    11. Quick check

    What does Adam's bias correction fix?
    E[mₜ] = (1 − β₁ᵗ)E[g]. Dividing by (1 − β₁ᵗ) removes the start-at-zero bias. It matters mostly for the first few hundred steps.
    A deep ReLU network is initialized with Xavier (σ² = 1/n for square layers). What happens to the activations?
    ReLU discards half the second moment, so the per-layer factor is n·(1/n)/2 = 1/2. He init (2/n) restores a factor of 1.
    In a transformer, LayerNorm computes its mean and variance over…
    Each token's d-dimensional vector is standardized on its own. No mixing across tokens or examples, and identical behavior in training and generation.
    Why warm up the learning rate at the start of training?
    Warmup is a stability device. Once the network and optimizer statistics have settled, large steps are safe.
