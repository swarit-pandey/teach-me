Part 2 · Calculus

Derivatives & the chain rule

Training a neural network means asking one question millions of times: "if I nudge this number a little, how much does the loss change?" That ratio is a derivative. The chain rule tells you how to get it through a long pipeline of functions, and that is all backpropagation is.

By the end you can

  • Define the derivative as the limit of secant slopes, and explain it as the best local linear approximation.
  • Derive the power, product, quotient, exp and log rules instead of memorising them.
  • Use the chain rule as "multiply the rates of change along the pipeline", and differentiate sigmoid, tanh and ReLU.
  • Estimate derivatives numerically, and explain why the error is smallest at a step size around $10^{-8}$ (forward) or $10^{-5}$ (central).
  • Run Newton's method and explain why its number of correct digits doubles every step.

    1. From average slope to instantaneous slope

    You already know the slope of a line: rise over run. A curve doesn't have a single slope, but you can ask for the average slope between two points on it. Pick a point $x$ and a step $h$. The line through $(x, f(x))$ and $(x+h, f(x+h))$ is a secant, and its slope is the difference quotient

    $$\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}.$$

    Now shrink $h$. The second point slides along the curve toward the first, and the secant swings around until it just grazes the curve at $x$. That limiting line is the tangent, and its slope is the derivative:

    $$\boxed{\;f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\;}$$
    Try it: drag the blue point along the curve and slide $h$ toward 0 (or press "shrink h"). The orange secant converges to the green tangent. Watch the readout: the gap between the secant slope and $f'(x)$ shrinks roughly in proportion to $h$. Try a negative $h$ too: the secant approaches the same tangent from the other side.

    Notation varies, and you'll see all of these: $f'(x)$, $\dfrac{df}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}f(x)$. Leibniz's $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is the most suggestive: "a tiny change in $y$ divided by the tiny change in $x$ that caused it". It's not literally a fraction, but it behaves like one often enough that the notation earns its keep (you'll see this in the chain rule).

    Worked example: $f(x) = x^3$ at $x = 2$

    Expand the numerator: $(2+h)^3 = 8 + 12h + 6h^2 + h^3$. So

    $$\frac{(2+h)^3 - 8}{h} = \frac{12h + 6h^2 + h^3}{h} = 12 + 6h + h^2.$$

    At $h = 0.1$ the secant slope is $12.61$. At $h = 0.01$ it is $12.0601$. As $h\to0$ the extra terms vanish and $f'(2) = 12$. Notice the error $6h + h^2$ is dominated by $6h$: it is proportional to $h$. Hold on to that; it comes back in section 7.

    2. The derivative is the best linear approximation

    Here's the view of the derivative that generalises to everything in the rest of the course (gradients in lesson 06, Jacobians in lesson 07). Rearrange the definition. For small $h$,

    $$f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h.$$

    The right-hand side is a straight line in $h$. Among all lines through $(x, f(x))$, the tangent is the one whose error shrinks faster than $h$: the error divided by $h$ goes to zero. Any other slope $m$ leaves an error of about $(f'(x) - m)h$, which only shrinks as fast as $h$ itself.

    Geometrically: zoom in far enough on a smooth curve and it looks straight. The line it looks like is the tangent.

    Try it: slide the zoom. The window is always centred on the point, and the shaded sliver is the gap between the curve and the tangent. Each 10× zoom shrinks the window 10×, but the worst gap shrinks about 100×. So relative to the window, the curve becomes indistinguishable from its tangent. Move $x$ anywhere: this curve is smooth everywhere, so it straightens out everywhere. That is what "differentiable" means. (A kink like the one in $|x|$ at 0 never straightens, however far you zoom.)

    The gap shrinks like $h^2$ because the next term of the approximation is $\tfrac12 f''(x)h^2$ (Taylor's theorem). The second derivative $f''$ measures curvature: how fast the slope itself changes. Lesson 06 extends this to the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives, and it explains why some learning rates diverge.

    3. The derivative is itself a function

    Compute $f'(x)$ at every $x$ and you get a new function $f'$. Its graph tells you, at a glance, where $f$ rises ($f'>0$), falls ($f'\lt 0$), and flattens out ($f'=0$). Flat points are where minima and maxima live, which is why optimisation is the hunt for zeros of the derivative.

    Try it: drag the point on the top plot. The tangent's slope on top is the height of $f'$ directly below. Find where $f'$ crosses zero and check that $f$ has a peak or valley there. Then switch to $|x|$: at the kink the slope jumps, so $f'$ is undefined at 0. This is what ReLU looks like too (section 6).

    4. The rules, derived

    You rarely evaluate the limit by hand. Instead you use a handful of rules, each a two-line consequence of the definition. Knowing why they hold means you can rebuild any you forget.

    Linearity

    $(af + bg)' = af' + bg'$. The limit of a sum is the sum of the limits. Differentiation is a linear operator, the same kind of object as a matrix in lesson 02.

    Power rule: $(x^n)' = n x^{n-1}$

    By the binomial theorem, $(x+h)^n = x^n + n x^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h^2 + \cdots$. Subtract $x^n$ and divide by $h$: every term but $n x^{n-1}$ still carries an $h$, so it vanishes in the limit. (This argument needs $n$ to be a positive integer. The rule holds for every real $n$, including $(\sqrt x)' = \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$ and $(1/x)' = -1/x^2$; the log trick below proves it.)

    Product rule: $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$

    Picture $f(x)\,g(x)$ as the area of a rectangle with sides $f$ and $g$. Nudge $x$: the sides grow by $\Delta f \approx f'h$ and $\Delta g \approx g'h$. The new area adds two thin strips, $g\,\Delta f$ and $f\,\Delta g$, plus a tiny corner $\Delta f\,\Delta g$ of size $h^2$, which disappears after dividing by $h$. What's left is $f'g + fg'$.

    Exponential: $(e^x)' = e^x$

    $\dfrac{e^{x+h} - e^x}{h} = e^x\cdot\dfrac{e^h - 1}{h}$. The fraction on the right doesn't depend on $x$ at all; it's the slope of $e^x$ at 0. The number $e \approx 2.71828$ is defined as the base that makes that slope exactly 1. So $e^x$ is the function that is its own derivative: its growth rate equals its value. More generally $(a^x)' = a^x \ln a$.

    Logarithm: $(\ln x)' = 1/x$

    Let $y = \ln x$, so $x = e^y$. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, using the chain rule (section 5) on the right: $1 = e^y\, y'$. So $y' = 1/e^y = 1/x$. Now the general power rule: $x^n = e^{n\ln x}$, so $(x^n)' = e^{n\ln x}\cdot \tfrac{n}{x} = n x^{n-1}$ for any real $n$.

    Quotient rule: $(f/g)' = (f'g - fg')/g^2$

    Write $f/g = f\cdot g^{-1}$. Product rule, then the power rule plus chain rule on $g^{-1}$: $f'g^{-1} + f\cdot(-g^{-2}g') = (f'g - fg')/g^2$.

    $f(x)$$f'(x)$$f(x)$$f'(x)$
    $x^n$$n x^{n-1}$$\sin x$$\cos x$
    $e^x$$e^x$$\cos x$$-\sin x$
    $\ln x$$1/x$$|x|$$\operatorname{sign}(x)$, undefined at 0

    5. The chain rule: rates multiply

    Most functions in ML are compositions: a pipeline where each stage's output is the next stage's input. Write $u = g(x)$ and $y = f(u)$, so $y = f(g(x))$. How fast does $y$ change with $x$?

    Think in rates. If $u$ changes 3 times as fast as $x$, and $y$ changes 2 times as fast as $u$, then $y$ changes $2\times3 = 6$ times as fast as $x$. Rates of change multiply along a chain:

    $$\boxed{\;\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} = f'(g(x))\; g'(x)\;}$$

    Derivation. Use the linear approximation twice. Nudge $x$ by $h$: then $u$ moves by $k \approx g'(x)\,h$. A nudge of $k$ in $u$ moves $y$ by about $f'(u)\,k = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\,h$. Divide by $h$. $\blacksquare$

    Try it: three number lines on the same scale: $x$, then $u = g(x) = \tfrac12x^2 - 1$, then $y = f(u) = 2\sin u$. A small nudge $\Delta x$ (the arrow on the top line) gets stretched by $g'(x)$ on the way to the middle line, then by $f'(u)$ on the way to the bottom. Move $x$ and watch the arrow grow, shrink, or flip direction (a negative derivative reverses it). Shrink $\Delta x$ and the measured ratio $\Delta y/\Delta x$ converges to the product $f'(u)\,g'(x)$.

    The most common slip: writing $f'(x)\,g'(x)$. The outer derivative is evaluated at the inner value $u = g(x)$, not at $x$. In code this means: run the pipeline forward first and remember the intermediate values, because the backward pass needs them. That is exactly what an autograd engine stores (lesson 17).

    Worked example: the Gaussian bump $y = e^{-x^2/2}$

    Split it into stages: $u = -x^2/2$, then $y = e^u$.

    Local rates: $\dfrac{du}{dx} = -x$ and $\dfrac{dy}{du} = e^u$.

    Multiply: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = e^u\cdot(-x) = -x\,e^{-x^2/2}$.

    At $x = 1$: $u = -0.5$, $e^{-0.5} \approx 0.6065$, so $y'(1) \approx -0.6065$. The bump is falling there, as it should be to the right of its peak. At $x = 0$ the derivative is $0$: the peak.

    Longer chains just keep multiplying. For $y = f_3(f_2(f_1(x)))$ with intermediate values $u_1 = f_1(x)$, $u_2 = f_2(u_1)$:

    $$\frac{dy}{dx} = f_3'(u_2)\cdot f_2'(u_1)\cdot f_1'(x).$$

    A deep network is a chain of 10, 100 or 1000 stages. Its derivative is a product of that many factors, which is why the size of each factor matters so much (next section).

    6. Derivatives of activation functions

    Neural networks (lesson 16) alternate linear maps with simple nonlinear functions called activations. You will differentiate these constantly, so derive them once now.

    Sigmoid $\sigma(z) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$. Write it as $(1+e^{-z})^{-1}$ and apply the chain rule:

    $$\sigma'(z) = -(1+e^{-z})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-z}) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} = \sigma(z)\,\big(1-\sigma(z)\big).$$

    The last step uses $\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} = 1 - \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$. The result is lovely for code: once you've computed $\sigma(z)$ on the forward pass, the derivative costs one multiply.

    Tanh $\tanh z = \dfrac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}}$ has $\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2 z$ (problem 4). ReLU $\max(0, z)$ has derivative $1$ for $z>0$ and $0$ for $z\lt 0$. At exactly $0$ it is undefined; frameworks just pick $0$, and since an input lands on exactly $0.0$ with negligible probability, it doesn't matter.

    Try it: pick an activation and drag $z$. The solid curve is the activation and the orange curve its derivative. Notice that sigmoid's derivative never exceeds $0.25$ and is nearly $0$ once $|z| > 5$ ("saturation"). Now raise "layers": the readout shows the best case for the product of that many sigmoid derivatives, $0.25^n$.

    This is the vanishing gradient problem. Stack 20 sigmoid layers and the chain rule multiplies 20 factors each at most $0.25$: the gradient reaching the first layer is under $10^{-12}$ of the signal, so early layers barely learn. ReLU's derivative is exactly 1 on its active side, which is a big reason deep networks switched to it (lesson 16), together with careful initialisation and normalisation (lesson 18) and residual connections (lesson 23).

    7. Numerical derivatives and floating-point error

    You can always estimate a derivative by evaluating the difference quotient at a small $h$. This is how you test hand-derived gradients (the "gradient check" you'll do in lessons 07 and 17). Two versions:

    $$\text{forward: } \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}, \qquad \text{central: } \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}.$$

    Truncation error. Taylor's theorem says $f(x\pm h) = f(x) \pm h f'(x) + \tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(x) \pm \tfrac{h^3}{6}f'''(x) + \cdots$. Plug in:

    $$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} = f'(x) + \underbrace{\tfrac{h}{2}f''(x)}_{O(h)} + \cdots, \qquad \frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h} = f'(x) + \underbrace{\tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''(x)}_{O(h^2)} + \cdots$$

    In the central version the $h^2$ terms of the two expansions are equal and cancel. So shrinking $h$ by 10× cuts the forward error 10× but the central error 100×.

    Rounding error. Shrinking $h$ forever doesn't work. A float64 stores about 16 significant digits (machine epsilon $\varepsilon \approx 2.2\times10^{-16}$). For tiny $h$, $f(x+h)$ and $f(x)$ agree in almost all of their digits, the subtraction cancels them, and what's left is mostly rounding noise of size about $\varepsilon|f(x)|$. Dividing by $h$ amplifies it: the rounding error behaves like $\varepsilon|f|/h$. Total error for forward differences:

    $$E(h) \approx \frac{|f''|}{2}\,h + \frac{\varepsilon|f|}{h}.$$

    One term falls with $h$ and the other rises, so there is a sweet spot. Setting $E'(h)=0$ gives $h^* = \sqrt{2\varepsilon|f|/|f''|}$, around $10^{-8}$ when $f$ and $f''$ are about the same size. For central differences the same reasoning gives $h^*\sim\varepsilon^{1/3}\approx 10^{-5}$ and a far smaller minimum error.

    Try it: a log–log plot of the actual error of each estimate, computed live in your browser's float64 arithmetic. Read it right to left (shrinking $h$). On the right, truncation dominates: the forward curve falls with slope 1 and the central curve with slope 2 (compare the dashed guides). On the left, rounding takes over and both rise with slope −1, ragged because rounding is noisy. Move the $h$ slider to read off exact digits, and find each method's best $h$.

    Rules of thumb for gradient checks in float64: central differences with $h\approx10^{-5}$ give about 10 correct digits. In float32 (what GPUs use), $\varepsilon\approx1.2\times10^{-7}$, so the best you can hope for is about 4–5 digits. That's why gradient checks are always run in float64.

    8. Newton's method: using the tangent to find roots

    Here is the derivative put to work. You want a root of $f$, a point where $f(x) = 0$. Start with a guess $x_0$. Replace $f$ by its tangent line at $x_0$, which is easy to solve: $f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0) = 0$. Its root is the next guess:

    $$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.$$

    Near a root (with $f'\neq0$ there), this converges quadratically: if the error is $e_n$, the next error is about $\frac{|f''|}{2|f'|}e_n^2$. Errors go $10^{-2}\to10^{-4}\to10^{-8}\to10^{-16}$: the number of correct digits roughly doubles every step.

    Try it: drag the starting guess $x_0$ along the axis, then press "step" (or "play"). Each step draws the tangent and slides down to where it hits zero. For $x^2-2$, watch the readout: the correct digits of $\sqrt2$ double. Then break it: $x^3-2x+2$ from $x_0=0$ cycles forever between 0 and 1, and $\arctan x$ from $|x_0|>1.4$ overshoots further every step.
    Worked example: $\sqrt2$ by hand

    $f(x) = x^2 - 2$, $f'(x) = 2x$, so $x_{n+1} = x_n - \dfrac{x_n^2 - 2}{2x_n} = \dfrac{x_n}{2} + \dfrac{1}{x_n}$.

    From $x_0 = 1$: $x_1 = 0.5 + 1 = 1.5$; $x_2 = 0.75 + 0.6667 = 1.41667$; $x_3 = 1.414216$; $x_4 = 1.41421356237469$.

    Errors: $8.6\times10^{-2}$, $2.5\times10^{-3}$, $2.1\times10^{-6}$, $1.6\times10^{-12}$. Each is roughly the square of the previous one (times $\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}\approx0.35$). This is the "Babylonian" square-root algorithm, nearly 4000 years old.

    To minimise a loss, you want a root of its derivative. Newton on $f'$ gives $x \leftarrow x - f'(x)/f''(x)$: jump to the bottom of the local parabola. In many dimensions $f''$ becomes the Hessian matrix (lesson 06), which is far too big to form for a network with a billion parameters. That's why deep learning uses gradient descent (lesson 06) and its cheaper cousins like Adam (lesson 18), which use slopes only.

    9. Problems

    Pencil and paper first. Use the figures to check your answers.

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    Let $f(x) = 3x^4 - 2x + 5$. Compute $f'(2)$.

    Solution

    Power rule and linearity: $f'(x) = 12x^3 - 2$. At $x=2$: $12\cdot 8 - 2 = 94$. The constant 5 contributes nothing: shifting a graph up doesn't change its slopes.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    Differentiate $f(x) = x^2 e^x$ and evaluate $f'(1)$.

    Solution

    Product rule: $f'(x) = 2x\,e^x + x^2 e^x = (x^2 + 2x)e^x$. At $x=1$: $3e \approx 8.1548$.

    Problem 3
    core

    The softplus function is $s(x) = \ln(1 + e^x)$, a smooth version of ReLU. Show that $s'(x) = \sigma(x)$, the sigmoid. Then evaluate $s'(2)$.

    Solution

    Chain rule with outer $\ln u$ and inner $u = 1 + e^x$: $s'(x) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^x}\cdot e^x = \dfrac{e^x}{1+e^x}$. Multiply top and bottom by $e^{-x}$: $\dfrac{1}{e^{-x} + 1} = \sigma(x)$. So $s'(2) = \sigma(2) = 1/(1+e^{-2}) \approx 0.8808$. You'll meet softplus again inside the logistic loss (lesson 14): its derivative being a sigmoid is no coincidence.

    Problem 4
    core · proof

    (a) Using the quotient rule, prove $\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2 z$. (b) Show that $\tanh z = 2\sigma(2z) - 1$, and use it with the chain rule to get $\tanh'(z) = 4\sigma'(2z)$. Check the two answers agree at $z=0$.

    Hint

    (a) Let $a = e^z - e^{-z}$ and $b = e^z + e^{-z}$. Then $a' = b$ and $b' = a$. (b) Write $\sigma(2z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-2z}}$ and multiply the top and bottom of $\tanh$ by $e^{z}$.

    Solution

    (a) $\tanh = a/b$ with $a'=b$, $b'=a$: $\tanh' = \dfrac{b\cdot b - a\cdot a}{b^2} = 1 - \dfrac{a^2}{b^2} = 1 - \tanh^2 z$.

    (b) $2\sigma(2z) - 1 = \dfrac{2}{1+e^{-2z}} - 1 = \dfrac{1 - e^{-2z}}{1 + e^{-2z}}$. Multiply top and bottom by $e^z$: $\dfrac{e^z - e^{-z}}{e^z + e^{-z}} = \tanh z$. ✓ Chain rule: $\tanh'(z) = 2\cdot\sigma'(2z)\cdot 2 = 4\sigma'(2z)$. At $z = 0$: (a) gives $1 - 0 = 1$; (b) gives $4\cdot\sigma(0)(1-\sigma(0)) = 4\cdot0.25 = 1$. ✓ So tanh is just a rescaled, recentred sigmoid with a maximum slope 4× larger.

    Problem 5
    core

    Estimate the derivative of $e^x$ at $x=0$ with $h = 0.1$, using (a) the forward difference and (b) the central difference. Enter the central estimate. Then predict both errors at $h = 0.01$ without computing, and check with a calculator.

    Solution

    (a) $(e^{0.1} - 1)/0.1 \approx 1.05171$, error $\approx 0.0517 \approx \tfrac{h}{2}f''(0) = 0.05$. (b) $(e^{0.1} - e^{-0.1})/0.2 \approx 1.0016675$, error $\approx 1.67\times10^{-3} \approx \tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''(0) = 0.001\overline{6}$.

    At $h = 0.01$: forward error ÷10 ≈ $0.0050$ (actual $0.005017$), central error ÷100 ≈ $1.67\times10^{-5}$ (actual $1.6667\times10^{-5}$). The leading Taylor term predicts the error almost exactly.

    Problem 6
    core

    A single sigmoid neuron computes $y = \sigma(wx + b)$. With $w = 2$, $x = 1.5$, $b = -1$, compute $\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial w}$ (treat $x$ and $b$ as constants).

    Hint

    Stages: $z = wx + b$, then $y = \sigma(z)$. Find $dz/dw$, $dy/dz$, multiply.

    Solution

    $z = 2(1.5) - 1 = 2$. $\sigma(2) \approx 0.88080$, so $\sigma'(2) = 0.88080\times0.11920 \approx 0.10499$. And $dz/dw = x = 1.5$. Product: $0.10499\times 1.5 \approx 0.15749$. Notice the factor $x$: the gradient for a weight is proportional to the input it multiplies. You'll see this "input times upstream gradient" pattern for every linear layer in lesson 07.

    Problem 7
    core

    Run two steps of Newton's method on $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ from $x_0 = 1$ and enter $x_2$. Then run Newton on $f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2$ from $x_0 = 0$ and explain what happens.

    Solution

    $x_1 = 1.5$, $x_2 = 17/12 \approx 1.416667$. For the cubic, $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2$: $x_1 = 0 - \frac{2}{-2} = 1$, then $x_2 = 1 - \frac{1}{1} = 0$. Back where we started: a 2-cycle that never converges. Newton's quadratic convergence is a local guarantee. Start far from a root and anything can happen.

    Problem 8
    stretch

    Use the error model $E(h) = \tfrac{|f''|}{2}h + \tfrac{\varepsilon|f|}{h}$ for the forward difference of $e^x$ at $x=0$ (so $f = f'' = 1$), with $\varepsilon = 2.22\times10^{-16}$. Find the optimal $h^*$ and enter $\log_{10} h^*$.

    Hint

    Set $E'(h) = \tfrac12 - \varepsilon/h^2 = 0$.

    Solution

    $h^* = \sqrt{2\varepsilon} \approx 2.1\times10^{-8}$, so $\log_{10}h^* \approx -7.68$. The minimum error is $E(h^*) = \sqrt{2\varepsilon}\approx 2\times10^{-8}$: even at its best, forward differencing keeps only about half of float64's 16 digits. Compare with section 7's plot (the measured optimum is noisy but lands near $10^{-8}$).

    Problem 9
    stretch

    Let $y = \sigma(\sigma(\cdots\sigma(x)\cdots))$ with 10 sigmoids nested. Give the tightest upper bound on $|dy/dx|$ that follows from $\sigma' \le 0.25$. (You may type an expression like 0.25^10.) Is the true value at $x = 0$ larger or smaller than this bound, and why?

    Solution

    The chain rule gives a product of 10 factors $\sigma'(u_k)$, each $\le 0.25$, so $|dy/dx| \le 0.25^{10} \approx 9.5\times10^{-7}$. The true value is smaller: only the innermost sigmoid sees input 0. Its output $0.5$ feeds the next one, whose derivative is $\sigma'(0.5) \approx 0.235$, and later layers see inputs near $0.65$ where $\sigma' \approx 0.22$. Every factor after the first is strictly below 0.25.

    10. Quick check

    You halve $h$ in a central difference, staying well above the rounding-error zone. The error…
    The central error is $\tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''$, so it scales with $h^2$: halving $h$ quarters it. Halving is the forward difference's behaviour. Doubling is what happens in the rounding regime, far to the left on the log–log plot.
    A colleague's gradient check uses a forward difference with $h = 10^{-15}$ and reports a 30% mismatch. The most likely explanation is…
    At $h=10^{-15}$ the rounding term $\varepsilon|f|/h \approx 0.2|f|$ swamps everything. Use a central difference with $h\approx10^{-5}$ before blaming the math.
    What is $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin(x^2)$?
    Outer derivative at the inner value: $\cos(u)$ with $u=x^2$, times the inner derivative $2x$. Option (b) evaluates the outer derivative at $x$ instead of at $u$, the classic slip.
    $f'(x_0) = 0$. What can you conclude?
    A zero derivative only says the best linear approximation is flat. Telling min from max from inflection needs second-order information: $f''$ here, and the Hessian's eigenvalues in lesson 06, where the "neither" case becomes a saddle point.
