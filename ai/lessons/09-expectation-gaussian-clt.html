Part 3 · Probability & statistics

Expectation, variance, Gaussians & the CLT

A distribution is a whole function. Most of the time you only need two numbers from it: where it sits (the mean) and how wide it is (the variance). This lesson builds those two numbers, the covariance that ties variables together, the Gaussian that they define, and the central limit theorem, which explains why the Gaussian turns up everywhere.

By the end you can

  • Compute expectations and variances, and use linearity of expectation and the variance scaling rules without thinking.
  • Explain covariance and correlation geometrically, and say why "uncorrelated" does not mean "independent".
  • Derive why the sample variance divides by $n-1$.
  • Read a Gaussian's $\mu,\sigma$ (or $\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma$) off a picture, standardize a value, and sample a multivariate normal with a Cholesky factor.
  • State the central limit theorem and use the standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$ to reason about minibatch noise.

    1. Expectation: the balance point

    In lesson 08 a random variable $X$ came with a PMF $p(x)$: the probability of each value. The expectation (or mean) is the probability-weighted average of the values:

    $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x) \qquad\text{(discrete)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\,p(x)\,dx \qquad\text{(continuous)}.$$

    Physically, put a weight of $p(x)$ at position $x$ on a weightless ruler. $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is the point where the ruler balances. That's exact, not a metaphor: the balance condition is that the torques cancel, $\sum_x (x - m)\,p(x) = 0$, and solving for $m$ gives $m = \sum_x x\,p(x)$.

    Try it: drag the dots on top of the bars to reshape the distribution (heights are re-normalized to sum to 1; the small numbers are the probabilities). The orange triangle is the balance point $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and the pink bracket spans $\mathbb{E}[X]\pm\sigma$. Pull one far-right bar up a little and watch the mean move a lot: the mean is sensitive to outliers. Try the "two humps" preset: the mean can sit where there's almost no probability.

    Two more facts you will use constantly. First, the expectation of a function of $X$ just reweights that function's values:

    $$\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\,p(x).$$

    Second, linearity of expectation: for any random variables $X, Y$ (dependent or not!) and constants $a, b, c$,

    $$\mathbb{E}[aX + bY + c] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y] + c.$$

    The proof is one line. Sum over the joint distribution and split the sum: $\sum_{x,y}(ax + by + c)\,p(x,y) = a\sum_{x,y} x\,p(x,y) + b\sum_{x,y} y\,p(x,y) + c\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)$. Summing out $y$ from $p(x,y)$ gives $p(x)$, so the first term is $a\,\mathbb{E}[X]$, the second is $b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$, and the last is $c\cdot1$. Nothing in that argument used independence.

    Linearity does not extend to non-linear functions: in general $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] \ne g(\mathbb{E}[X])$. For a fair die, $\mathbb{E}[X]^2 = 12.25$ but $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 91/6 \approx 15.17$. The gap between those two numbers is exactly the variance (next section).

    2. Variance and standard deviation

    The mean says where the mass sits. The variance says how spread out it is: the expected squared distance from the mean,

    $$\operatorname{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu)^2\big], \qquad \mu = \mathbb{E}[X].$$

    Squared distance has squared units (meters²), so we usually quote the standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[X]}$, which is in the same units as $X$. Why squares and not $\mathbb{E}|X - \mu|$? Squares are smooth (differentiable everywhere, as lesson 05 likes), they add up nicely for independent variables (section 3), and they make the Gaussian come out naturally.

    Expand the square and use linearity to get the shortcut formula:

    $$\operatorname{Var}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X] + \mu^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2.$$

    And the two scaling rules, both immediate from the definition:

    $$\operatorname{Var}[X + c] = \operatorname{Var}[X], \qquad \operatorname{Var}[aX] = a^2\operatorname{Var}[X], \qquad\text{so}\quad \sigma_{aX + c} = |a|\,\sigma_X.$$

    Shifting moves the distribution without widening it; scaling by $a$ stretches every distance from the mean by $|a|$, so squared distances grow by $a^2$.

    Worked example: a fair die

    $\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6} = \frac{21}{6} = 3.5$.

    $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \frac{1+4+9+16+25+36}{6} = \frac{91}{6} \approx 15.167$.

    $\operatorname{Var}[X] = \frac{91}{6} - 3.5^2 = \frac{91}{6} - \frac{147}{12} = \frac{182 - 147}{12} = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.917$, so $\sigma \approx 1.708$.

    Now let $Y = 10X - 5$ (a die with relabeled faces). Without recomputing anything: $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 10(3.5) - 5 = 30$ and $\sigma_Y = 10 \times 1.708 = 17.08$.

    The shortcut $\mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2$ is exact algebra but bad numerics. If your data are around $10^9$ with spread $0.1$, both terms are about $10^{18}$ and their difference drowns in float rounding. Code should subtract the mean first and then square ("two-pass"). The workbook tests for this.

    3. Covariance and correlation

    With two variables, we want to know whether they move together. The covariance is the expected product of their deviations from their means:

    $$\operatorname{Cov}[X, Y] = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\big] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mu_X\mu_Y.$$

    Read the product's sign quadrant by quadrant. When $X$ is above its mean and $Y$ is above its mean, the product is positive. Both below: also positive. One above and one below: negative. Covariance is the average of those signed products. It is positive when points mostly fall in the "agree" quadrants and negative when they mostly fall in the "disagree" ones. Note $\operatorname{Cov}[X, X] = \operatorname{Var}[X]$.

    Covariance has units of $X$ times units of $Y$, so its size is hard to judge. Divide out the standard deviations and you get the unit-free correlation:

    $$\rho_{XY} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}[X, Y]}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y} \in [-1, 1].$$

    This should look familiar. Center a dataset and stack the $x$-deviations into a vector $\mathbf{a}$ and the $y$-deviations into $\mathbf{b}$. Then the sample covariance is $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}/n$, each variance is $\|\cdot\|^2/n$, and the correlation is exactly the cosine similarity $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}/(\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|)$ from lesson 01. That's why it lies in $[-1,1]$: Cauchy–Schwarz.

    Try it: slide $\rho$. Each point is colored by the sign of its contribution $(x-\bar x)(y-\bar y)$: blue adds to the covariance, pink subtracts. At $\rho = 0.8$ blue dominates; at $\rho = 0$ they cancel. Press "resample" a few times at $\rho = 0$: with 300 points the sample correlation wobbles around 0 by roughly $\pm0.06$. That wobble is sampling noise, the topic of section 8. Then choose "Y = X² (curved)": a perfect dependence, yet the correlation is about 0.

    Uncorrelated does not mean independent. Correlation only measures the linear part of a relationship. If $X$ is symmetric around 0 and $Y = X^2$, then $Y$ is completely determined by $X$, but $\operatorname{Cov}[X, Y] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0 - 0 = 0$. The converse does hold: independent ⇒ $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$ ⇒ covariance 0.

    Variance of a sum

    Expand $(X + Y - \mu_X - \mu_Y)^2$ and take expectations:

    $$\operatorname{Var}[X + Y] = \operatorname{Var}[X] + \operatorname{Var}[Y] + 2\operatorname{Cov}[X, Y].$$

    More generally $\operatorname{Var}[aX + bY] = a^2\operatorname{Var}[X] + b^2\operatorname{Var}[Y] + 2ab\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]$. When the variables are independent the cross term vanishes and variances add. Standard deviations don't add; variances do. The CLT and the $1/\sqrt n$ law below both come from this one fact.

    Worked example: two dice

    Let $S = X_1 + X_2$ be the sum of two independent fair dice. By linearity, $\mathbb{E}[S] = 3.5 + 3.5 = 7$. By independence, $\operatorname{Var}[S] = \frac{35}{12} + \frac{35}{12} = \frac{35}{6} \approx 5.833$, so $\sigma_S \approx 2.415$.

    Compare $T = 2X_1$ (one die, doubled). Same mean, 7, but $\operatorname{Var}[T] = 4 \cdot \frac{35}{12} = \frac{35}{3} \approx 11.667$. That's twice as much as $S$. $X_1$ is perfectly correlated with itself, so the cross term $2\operatorname{Cov}[X_1, X_1] = 2\operatorname{Var}[X_1]$ is fully present. Independent noise partly cancels; copies of the same noise never do.

    The covariance matrix

    For a random vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, collect every pairwise covariance into the $d \times d$ covariance matrix $\Sigma_{jk} = \operatorname{Cov}[x_j, x_k]$, or compactly $\Sigma = \mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top]$. It's symmetric, its diagonal holds the variances, and it is positive semi-definite: for any direction $\mathbf{w}$, $\mathbf{w}^\top\Sigma\,\mathbf{w} = \operatorname{Var}[\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}] \ge 0$. With data in an $n\times d$ matrix $X$ with centered columns $X_c$, the sample version is one matmul: $\hat\Sigma = X_c^\top X_c / (n-1)$. That's the matrix whose eigenvectors were the principal components in lesson 04.

    4. From data: sample mean, sample variance, and n − 1

    In practice you don't know $p(x)$. You have $n$ samples $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and you estimate:

    $$\bar x = \frac1n\sum_i x_i, \qquad s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2.$$

    Why $n-1$ and not $n$? Because $\bar x$ was fitted to the same data, the data sit closer to $\bar x$ than to the true $\mu$ (in fact $\bar x$ is the number that minimizes $\sum_i (x_i - c)^2$). Dividing by $n$ therefore underestimates the spread on average. Here is the exact amount. Write $x_i - \bar x = (x_i - \mu) - (\bar x - \mu)$ and expand:

    $$\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 = \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar x - \mu)^2.$$

    (The cross term is $-2(\bar x - \mu)\sum_i(x_i - \mu) = -2n(\bar x-\mu)^2$, which combines with the $+n(\bar x - \mu)^2$ from the last square.) Take expectations. Each $\mathbb{E}[(x_i - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$, and $\mathbb{E}[(\bar x - \mu)^2] = \operatorname{Var}[\bar x] = \sigma^2/n$ (section 8). So

    $$\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\Big] = n\sigma^2 - n\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{n} = (n-1)\,\sigma^2.$$

    Dividing by $n-1$ gives an estimator whose average is exactly $\sigma^2$: it's unbiased. Intuitively, one of the $n$ "degrees of freedom" was spent estimating the mean. This is Bessel's correction. NumPy's np.var defaults to dividing by $n$ (ddof=0) and np.cov to $n-1$, a classic source of confusion. For $n$ in the thousands, nobody cares. For $n = 5$, it's a 25% difference.

    5. The Gaussian

    The normal or Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has density

    $$p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big).$$

    Read it from the inside out. $(x-\mu)/\sigma$ measures distance from the center in units of $\sigma$. Squaring it and negating it inside $\exp$ gives a bump that falls off very fast: one $\sigma$ away the density is $e^{-1/2}\approx 0.61$ of the peak, three $\sigma$ away it's $e^{-4.5}\approx 0.011$. The constant $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$ just makes the area 1. Its mean is $\mu$ and its variance is $\sigma^2$, so the two parameters are exactly the two numbers from sections 1 and 2.

    Try it: move $\mu$ (shifts) and $\sigma$ (widens). The dashed curve is the standard normal $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ for reference. Shrink $\sigma$ and the peak climbs above 1: a density can exceed 1, only its area must equal 1. Switch the mode to "CDF" and drag the dot on the axis: the shaded area is $P(X \le x) = \Phi(z)$, and it depends only on the z-score $z = (x-\mu)/\sigma$.

    The 68–95–99.7 rule. A Gaussian puts about 68.3% of its mass within $1\sigma$ of the mean, 95.4% within $2\sigma$, and 99.7% within $3\sigma$. So "4σ away" is a roughly 1-in-16,000 event, and anything far out deserves suspicion.

    Standardization. If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ then $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. By the scaling rules, $Z$ has mean 0 and variance 1. A shifted and scaled Gaussian is still Gaussian (substitute into the density). So every Gaussian question becomes a question about one table: the standard normal CDF $\Phi$. Conversely, $X = \mu + \sigma Z$ is how you sample any Gaussian from a standard one.

    Worked example

    Adult heights in some population are roughly $\mathcal{N}(170, 10^2)$ cm. What fraction are taller than 190 cm?

    Standardize: $z = (190 - 170)/10 = 2$. So $P(X > 190) = P(Z > 2) = 1 - \Phi(2) \approx 1 - 0.97725 = 0.02275$. About 2.3%. Sanity check with the rule: 95.4% lie within $\pm 2\sigma$, the remaining 4.6% splits evenly between the two tails, giving 2.3% above. ✓

    Gaussians are everywhere in deep learning. Weights are initialized from $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ chosen to keep activations' variance stable (lesson 18, using exactly the "variances add" rule). Layer norm standardizes activations (lesson 18 again). Assuming Gaussian noise on the targets is what turns maximum likelihood into mean-squared-error (lesson 10). Diffusion models add and remove Gaussian noise.

    6. The multivariate Gaussian

    In $d$ dimensions, the Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)$ has density

    $$p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^d \det\Sigma}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\Big).$$

    It has the same shape as the 1-D formula: $(x-\mu)^2/\sigma^2$ became the quadratic form $(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)$, the squared Mahalanobis distance. Points with the same density are where that quadratic form is constant, and that set is an ellipse (an ellipsoid in higher dimensions).

    The ellipse's geometry is the eigen-decomposition of $\Sigma$ from lesson 04. $\Sigma = Q\Lambda Q^\top$, the eigenvectors (columns of $Q$) are the ellipse's axes, and the half-lengths of the 1-σ ellipse are $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. The first eigenvector is the direction of greatest variance: the first principal component. PCA is fitting a Gaussian and reading off its axes.

    Try it: set $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$ and $\rho$. That builds $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1^2 & \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\\ \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2\end{bmatrix}$. The ellipses are Mahalanobis distance 1, 2 and 3. The arrows are the eigenvectors scaled by $\sqrt{\lambda}$. With $\rho = 0$ the ellipse is axis-aligned. Push $\rho$ toward $\pm1$ and it collapses toward a line: $\det\Sigma \to 0$, the second eigenvalue vanishes, and the data become effectively 1-D. The 400 dots are the same standard-normal draws $\mathbf{z}$ mapped through $\mathbf{x} = L\mathbf{z}$, so you can watch each one move.

    Sampling with a Cholesky factor

    How do you draw from $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu, \Sigma)$ when your library only gives you independent standard normals? Use the 1-D trick $x = \mu + \sigma z$ with a matrix square root. If $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$ and $\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol\mu + L\mathbf{z}$, then

    $$\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf{x}] = \mathbb{E}[L\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^\top L^\top] = L\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^\top]\,L^\top = L I L^\top = LL^\top.$$

    So any $L$ with $LL^\top = \Sigma$ works. The standard choice is the Cholesky factor: the unique lower-triangular $L$ with a positive diagonal. It exists exactly when $\Sigma$ is positive definite. For 2×2 you can solve for it by hand.

    Worked example: Cholesky and ellipse of a 2×2 covariance

    Take $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$ and write $L = \begin{bmatrix}a & 0\\ b & c\end{bmatrix}$, so $LL^\top = \begin{bmatrix}a^2 & ab\\ ab & b^2 + c^2\end{bmatrix}$.

    Match entries: $a^2 = 2 \Rightarrow a = \sqrt2 \approx 1.414$. Then $ab = 1 \Rightarrow b = 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707$. Then $b^2 + c^2 = 2 \Rightarrow c = \sqrt{2 - 1/2} = \sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.225$.

    Ellipse: the eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ are $2 \pm 1$, so $\lambda_1 = 3$ along $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$ along $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$. The 1-σ ellipse is tilted 45° with half-axes $\sqrt3 \approx 1.732$ and $1$. The correlation is $\rho = 1/\sqrt{2\cdot2} = 0.5$.

    The equation $\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol\mu + L\mathbf{z}$ is the reparameterization trick. Writing a random sample as a deterministic, differentiable function of parameters plus parameter-free noise lets gradients flow through sampling. Variational autoencoders depend on it, and you'll see the same idea behind sampling layers generally.

    7. The central limit theorem

    Add up many independent random variables. Whatever their individual distribution (flat, skewed, discrete), the sum's distribution approaches a Gaussian. Precisely: if $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, and $S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n$, then

    $$Z_n = \frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n} \;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\; \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\quad\text{in distribution.}$$

    The centering and scaling are not mysterious. By linearity $\mathbb{E}[S_n] = n\mu$, and by "variances add" $\operatorname{Var}[S_n] = n\sigma^2$. So $Z_n$ is just $S_n$ standardized, and it always has mean 0 and variance 1. What the CLT adds is the shape: it becomes the bell curve.

    Try it: start with $n = 1$. The histogram is just the base distribution. Raise $n$ (the histogram resets and refills) and watch the sum turn into the red $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ curve. The flat uniform gets there by $n \approx 5$. The skewed exponential and the lopsided "rare event" Bernoulli(0.1) need much larger $n$: their asymmetry fades only like $1/\sqrt n$. With the Bernoulli you also see spikes, because the sum can only take integer values. The CLT is about the overall shape, not about gaps between points.

    Why a Gaussian, of all shapes? One way to see it: the Gaussian is a fixed point. A sum of two independent Gaussians is again Gaussian, so standardized sums of Gaussians stay exactly Gaussian. Adding many small independent pieces washes out every feature of the original shape except its mean and variance, and the Gaussian is the shape determined by just those two numbers. (Lesson 11 makes this sharp: among all distributions with a given variance, the Gaussian has maximum entropy.)

    The CLT needs finite variance and enough independence. Heavy-tailed variables (a Cauchy distribution, or financial returns in a crash) can break it: their averages never settle into a bell. Strongly correlated terms, such as consecutive tokens or consecutive steps of a training run, converge much more slowly than $n$ suggests.

    8. Standard error: the 1/√n law

    Divide the sum by $n$ to get the sample mean $\bar X = S_n / n$. By the scaling rule,

    $$\operatorname{Var}[\bar X] = \frac{1}{n^2}\operatorname{Var}[S_n] = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{SE} = \operatorname{std}[\bar X] = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.$$

    This is the standard error. Together with the CLT it says $\bar X \approx \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$, so $\bar x \pm 2\sigma/\sqrt n$ is a roughly 95% confidence interval for $\mu$. (In lesson 08 the law of large numbers said $\bar X \to \mu$. This says how fast.) The square root has teeth: to halve your error you need four times the data, and one more decimal digit of precision costs 100× the samples.

    Try it: each column holds 150 sample means of $n$ die rolls, with $n$ doubling from left to right. The curves mark $3.5 \pm 2\sigma/\sqrt n$. Every step right is 2× the data but shrinks the cloud only by $1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.71$, and it takes two steps to halve it. Press "resample" and compare the empirical spread with $\sigma/\sqrt n$ in the readout.

    Minibatch gradients are sample means. In stochastic gradient descent (lessons 06 and 18), the gradient of the loss over a minibatch of $B$ examples is the average of $B$ per-example gradients, an estimate of the full-dataset gradient. Its noise shrinks like $1/\sqrt B$. Going from $B = 64$ to $B = 256$ costs 4× the compute per step and only halves the gradient noise. That trade-off sits behind the batch-size and learning-rate scaling rules in large-model training. The same law sets how many prompts you need to evaluate an LLM before a 1-point accuracy difference means anything.

    9. Problems

    Pencil and paper first. Compute by hand, then check.

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    A biased coin shows heads with probability $p$. Let $X = 1$ for heads and $0$ for tails (a Bernoulli variable). Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\operatorname{Var}[X]$ as functions of $p$. For which $p$ is the variance largest? Enter the variance at $p = 0.3$.

    Solution

    $\mathbb{E}[X] = 1\cdot p + 0\cdot(1-p) = p$. Since $X^2 = X$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = p$, so $\operatorname{Var}[X] = p - p^2 = p(1-p)$. That's a downward parabola, largest at $p = 1/2$ (value $1/4$) and zero at $p \in \{0, 1\}$, where there's no uncertainty. At $p = 0.3$: $0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.21$.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    $\mathbb{E}[X] = 3$, $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 2$, $\operatorname{Var}[X] = 1$, $\operatorname{Var}[Y] = 4$, $\operatorname{Cov}[X, Y] = 0.5$. Compute $\mathbb{E}[2X - Y + 1]$ and $\operatorname{Var}[2X - Y + 1]$. Enter both, comma-separated.

    Solution

    Mean: $2(3) - 2 + 1 = 5$. Variance: the $+1$ doesn't matter. With $a = 2$, $b = -1$: $a^2\operatorname{Var}[X] + b^2\operatorname{Var}[Y] + 2ab\operatorname{Cov} = 4(1) + 1(4) + 2(2)(-1)(0.5) = 4 + 4 - 2 = 6$.

    Problem 3
    core

    Heights are $\mathcal{N}(170, 10^2)$ cm. (a) What fraction are taller than 190 cm? (b) What fraction lie between 150 and 190 cm? Enter both as probabilities, comma-separated (4 decimal places).

    Hint

    Standardize both endpoints. $\Phi(2) \approx 0.97725$.

    Solution

    (a) $z = 2$, so $1 - \Phi(2) \approx 0.02275$. (b) $z \in [-2, 2]$: $\Phi(2) - \Phi(-2) = 2\Phi(2) - 1 \approx 0.9545$.

    Problem 4
    core

    (a) What is the standard error of the mean of 100 fair-die rolls? (b) You estimate the probability that a model answers a benchmark question correctly. The true rate is about 0.5, so each question is a Bernoulli with $\sigma \approx 0.5$. How many questions do you need for a standard error of 0.01 (one percentage point)? Enter both, comma-separated.

    Solution

    (a) $\sigma/\sqrt n = \sqrt{35/12}/\sqrt{100} \approx 1.7078/10 \approx 0.1708$. (b) $0.5/\sqrt n = 0.01 \Rightarrow \sqrt n = 50 \Rightarrow n = 2500$. A 95% interval would still be $\pm 2$ points, so beware of leaderboards built on a few hundred questions.

    Problem 5
    core · proof

    Let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $Y = X^2$. (a) Show that $\operatorname{Cov}[X, Y] = 0$. (b) Show that $X$ and $Y$ are not independent by finding one pair of events that violates $P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)$.

    Solution

    (a) $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0$. $XY = X^3 = X$, so $\mathbb{E}[XY] = 0$. Then $\operatorname{Cov} = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0 - 0 = 0$.

    (b) $P(X = 0) = 1/3$ and $P(Y = 1) = 2/3$, but $P(X = 0, Y = 1) = 0 \ne 2/9$. Knowing $X$ tells you $Y$ exactly. The relationship is a perfect parabola, and correlation, which only sees straight lines, misses it.

    Problem 6
    core

    Find the Cholesky factor $L$ of $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 2\\ 2 & 5\end{bmatrix}$. Enter $L_{11}, L_{21}, L_{22}$. Then explain how you'd draw 1000 samples from $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma)$ given only standard_normal.

    Hint

    Match $LL^\top = \begin{bmatrix}a^2 & ab\\ ab & b^2+c^2\end{bmatrix}$ entry by entry, top-left first.

    Solution

    $a^2 = 4 \Rightarrow a = 2$. $ab = 2 \Rightarrow b = 1$. $b^2 + c^2 = 5 \Rightarrow c = 2$. So $L = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$. Draw $Z$ of shape $(1000, 2)$ from standard_normal and return $Z L^\top$. Each row is $L\mathbf{z}$, with covariance $LL^\top = \Sigma$.

    Problem 7
    core

    Your training loop uses minibatches of 64 and the gradient estimate is noisy. A colleague suggests batch size 256. Assuming per-example gradients are independent, by what factor does the standard deviation of each gradient component shrink?

    Solution

    SE $\propto 1/\sqrt B$, so the ratio is $\sqrt{256/64} = \sqrt4 = 2$. Four times the compute per step buys half the noise. In practice examples within a batch are not fully independent (e.g. sequences from the same document), so the real gain is often smaller.

    Problem 8
    stretch · proof

    Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. (a) Prove $\operatorname{Var}[\bar x] = \sigma^2/n$. (b) Prove the identity $\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 = \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar x - \mu)^2$. (c) Conclude that $\mathbb{E}\big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(x_i - \bar x)^2\big] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$. For $n = 5$, what fraction of the true variance does the divide-by-$n$ estimator recover on average?

    Hint

    (a) Independence makes the variance of a sum the sum of variances; then use $\operatorname{Var}[aX] = a^2\operatorname{Var}[X]$ with $a = 1/n$. (b) Write $x_i - \bar x = (x_i - \mu) - (\bar x - \mu)$ and use $\sum_i (x_i - \mu) = n(\bar x - \mu)$.

    Solution

    (a) $\operatorname{Var}[\sum x_i] = n\sigma^2$ by independence, so $\operatorname{Var}[\bar x] = n\sigma^2/n^2 = \sigma^2/n$.

    (b) $\sum_i [(x_i-\mu) - (\bar x-\mu)]^2 = \sum_i (x_i-\mu)^2 - 2(\bar x - \mu)\sum_i(x_i - \mu) + n(\bar x-\mu)^2 = \sum_i (x_i-\mu)^2 - 2n(\bar x-\mu)^2 + n(\bar x-\mu)^2$.

    (c) Expectation of the right side: $n\sigma^2 - n\cdot\sigma^2/n = (n-1)\sigma^2$. Divide by $n$: $\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$. For $n = 5$ that's $4/5 = 0.8$. The plug-in estimator is 20% low on average, which is why ddof=1 exists.

    Problem 9
    stretch

    A neuron computes $y = \sum_{i=1}^{d} w_i x_i$ with $d$ inputs. The $x_i$ are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, and the weights are independent of them and of each other, with mean 0 and variance $v$. (a) Show $\operatorname{Var}[y] = d\,v$. (b) What $v$ keeps $\operatorname{Var}[y] = 1$ when $d = 512$? Enter $v$.

    Hint

    For independent zero-mean $W$ and $X$: $\operatorname{Var}[WX] = \mathbb{E}[W^2X^2] = \mathbb{E}[W^2]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]$.

    Solution

    (a) Each term has mean 0 and variance $\mathbb{E}[w_i^2]\mathbb{E}[x_i^2] = v \cdot 1 = v$. The terms are independent, so variances add: $d\,v$. (b) $v = 1/d = 1/512 \approx 0.00195$, i.e. weight std $1/\sqrt{512} \approx 0.0442$. This is the core of Xavier/He initialization (lesson 18). Get it wrong and activations explode or vanish layer after layer.

    10. Quick check

    Two features in your dataset have correlation 0. What can you conclude?
    Correlation measures only the linear trend. $Y = X^2$ for symmetric $X$ is perfectly dependent with zero correlation. (Independence ⇒ uncorrelated, not the reverse. For jointly Gaussian variables the two happen to coincide.)
    $\operatorname{Var}[X] = 1$. What is $\operatorname{Var}[X + X]$?
    $X + X = 2X$, and $\operatorname{Var}[2X] = 4\operatorname{Var}[X]$. "Variances add" requires independence (zero covariance), and $X$ is perfectly correlated with itself. The cross term $2\operatorname{Cov}[X,X] = 2$ supplies the difference between 2 and 4.
    What does the central limit theorem actually say?
    The data themselves keep their shape. A histogram of a million exponential draws is still exponential. It's the distribution of sums or averages that becomes Gaussian. Option c confuses the CLT with the law of large numbers, and "30" is folklore.
    An A/B test with 10,000 users per arm gives a standard error of 0.4 percentage points. Roughly how many users per arm for 0.1 points?
    SE ∝ $1/\sqrt n$. To shrink it 4× you need $4^2 = 16$× the data: $16 \times 10{,}000 = 160{,}000$.
