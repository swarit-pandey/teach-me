Part 6 · Transformers

Train a tiny GPT

Time to put everything together and watch a transformer learn to write. You'll build the data pipeline, assemble a 0.8M-parameter character-level GPT, predict its loss before training, then train it on Shakespeare with AdamW on your laptop's CPU. Along the way you'll watch random characters turn into something that looks like a play. The loss curve and samples on this page come from a run of the provided train.py.

By the end you can

  • Build a language-model data pipeline: vocabulary, encoding, a leak-free train/validation split, and random context windows with shifted targets.
  • Predict a model's loss at initialization ($\ln V$), derive the correction for non-tiny initial logits, and use it to debug.
  • Write and read a full training loop (AdamW, warm-up + cosine schedule, gradient clipping, eval loss) and interpret train/val curves against simple baselines.
  • Generate text autoregressively, and connect cross-entropy to bits per character and compression.

    1. The plan

    Tiny Shakespeare is about 1.1 million characters of plays, one text file. We'll train a model that, given up to 128 characters, predicts the next one. That's the bigram/MLP/RNN task from lesson 20, now with the transformer from lesson 23. Four pieces:

    1. Data: text → ids → batches of (input, target) windows (section 2).
    2. Model: token + position embeddings, 4 transformer blocks, LayerNorm, linear head (section 3).
    3. Training: cross-entropy, AdamW, a learning-rate schedule, evaluation on held-out text (sections 4–5).
    4. Sampling: feed the model its own output, one character at a time (section 6).

    We use characters as tokens ($V = 65$) instead of BPE (lesson 21). The vocabulary is tiny, there is no tokenizer to train, and every sample is easy to read. The cost is long sequences: 128 tokens are only about 25 words of context.

    2. The data pipeline

    Vocabulary. Collect the distinct characters, sort them, and number them: itos is the sorted list, stoi the inverse map. Tiny Shakespeare has 65: newline, space, the 52 upper- and lower-case letters, and 11 symbols (punctuation, a stray dollar sign, and the digit 3, the only digit in the file). The whole corpus becomes one long int64 tensor data.

    Train/validation split. Take the first 90% for training and the last 10% for validation, as contiguous blocks. Don't shuffle characters, or windows, into the two sets: neighbouring windows overlap almost entirely. A validation window starting one character after a training window would be 99% memorized text, and the validation loss would lie. A contiguous split means validation is genuinely unseen text (a different play, in fact).

    Batches. Pick $B$ random start positions $i$. The input is data[i : i+T] and the target is data[i+1 : i+T+1], the same window shifted by one. Thanks to the causal mask (lesson 22), each window is $T$ training examples at once: position $t$ is trained to predict character $t+1$ from characters $1..t$, for every $t$.

Every column is one supervised example: "given everything up to here, predict the box below".
    Worked example: how much data is a run?

    The training split has $\lfloor 0.9 \times 1{,}115{,}394\rfloor = 1{,}003{,}854$ characters. One step with $B = 32$, $T = 128$ makes $32 \times 128 = 4096$ predictions. train.py runs 3,000 steps: $12{,}288{,}000$ predictions, or about $12.2$ passes ("epochs") over the training text. Each window is a fresh random draw, so the model sees every character in many different contexts and positions.

    3. The model

    Exactly the GPT of lesson 23, small:

    SettingValueParameters
    vocabulary $V$ / context $T$65 / 128token emb $65\cdot128 = 8{,}320$; position emb $128\cdot128 = 16{,}384$
    width $d$, heads $h$128, 4 (so $d_h = 32$)
    layers $L$4$4\,(12\cdot128^2 + 13\cdot128) = 793{,}088$
    final LN + head (untied)$256 + 8{,}320$
    total826,368

    Two initialization details from GPT-2 matter more than you'd think. Every weight starts as $\mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)$, and the two layers that write into the residual stream (attention's output projection and the MLP's second layer) are scaled down further by $1/\sqrt{2L}$. That keeps the stream's variance from growing with depth at initialization (lesson 23, section 2).

    4. What should the loss be at step 0?

    Before training, predict the number your code will print. That one number catches a surprising fraction of bugs.

    With small random weights the logits are all close to 0, so the softmax is close to uniform: every character gets probability $1/V$. Cross-entropy (lesson 11) is $-\log p(\text{correct})$, so

    $$\mathcal L_0 \approx -\ln\frac1V = \ln V = \ln 65 \approx 4.174 \text{ nats}.$$

    That's the loss of knowing nothing. If your first printed loss is 4.17, the wiring is plausible. If it's 0.5, you're probably leaking the target into the input (a missing causal mask, or targets not shifted). If it's 10, the initialization is too large.

    Why a large init is worse than "knowing nothing". Suppose the initial logits are independent $\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)$. The loss is $\log\sum_j e^{z_j} - z_y$, and $\mathbb E[z_y] = 0$. For large $V$, the sum is close to $V\,\mathbb E[e^{z}]$, and the mean of a log-normal is $\mathbb E[e^z] = e^{\sigma^2/2}$ (lesson 09). So

    $$\mathbb E[\mathcal L_0] \approx \ln\!\big(V e^{\sigma^2/2}\big) = \ln V + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}.$$

    Random but confident predictions are confidently wrong, and each unit of logit variance costs half a nat. The first few hundred training steps then go into merely shrinking the logits (the "hockey stick" at the start of many loss curves) instead of learning anything. GPT-2's $0.02$ init keeps $\sigma \approx 0.02\sqrt d \approx 0.23$, costing only about $0.03$ nats.

    Try it: slide the standard deviation $\sigma$ of the initial logits. Left: the expected initial loss (Monte Carlo, solid), the approximation $\ln V + \sigma^2/2$ (dashed), and the floor $\ln 65$. Right: the predicted distribution over the 65 characters for one random input, with the correct character in orange. At $\sigma = 0.2$ the prediction is nearly flat ("I don't know"). At $\sigma = 3$ it puts most of its mass on a few arbitrary characters ("I'm sure, and wrong").

    5. The training loop

    The core of train.py is the same five lines you've written since lesson 19:

    for step in range(steps):
    x, y = get_batch(train_data, T, B)          # (B, T) each
    logits, loss = model(x, y)                  # mean cross-entropy over B*T predictions
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    opt.step()

    Around it are three ingredients from lesson 18:

    • AdamW with $\beta = (0.9, 0.99)$ and weight decay $0.1$ applied only to weight matrices and embeddings. Decaying LayerNorm gains or biases toward 0 would just fight the normalization.
    • Learning-rate schedule: linear warm-up for 100 steps (Adam's second-moment estimates are noisy at first, so big early steps are risky), then cosine decay from $2\times10^{-3}$ to $2\times10^{-4}$. Small steps at the end let the model settle into a minimum instead of bouncing around it.
    • Gradient clipping to global norm 1: a rare bad batch can't throw the weights far.

    Evaluation. Every 250 steps we estimate the loss on 20 random batches from both splits, under torch.no_grad() and model.eval() (no graph, and no dropout if there were any). The batch loss printed each step is too noisy for decisions; the averaged train and validation losses are what you watch.

    Try it: a real run of train.py on a laptop CPU. Faint dots: single-batch losses. Lines: averaged train and validation loss. Slide through the steps. The model passes the bigram level within a few hundred steps and the trigram level soon after.     Always compare a loss to baselines. "Val loss 1.6" means nothing alone. "Uniform 4.17, unigram 3.35, bigram 2.48, trigram 2.05, ours 1.53" tells you the model uses far more than the last two characters of context.

    6. Sampling

    Generation (workbook 24's generate) starts from a prompt, here a single newline. Then it loops: crop the context to the last $T$ characters, run the model, take the logits at the last position, softmax (optionally with a temperature, lesson 25), sample a character, append it. The model never sees the "real" next character during generation. It conditions on its own previous choices, so small errors can compound.

    Try it: slide through the training run. Each text is 200 characters sampled at temperature 1 from the model checkpoint at that step, always with the same random seed. At step 0 the output is uniform noise over the 65 characters. Within ~100 steps it learns the character frequencies (lots of spaces and e's) and line breaks. Next come word-like chunks and the play format (NAME:, newlines). Late in training it produces real words, plausible names and a recognizable rhythm, though the sentences still don't mean anything.

    7. What the number means: bits and compression

    A loss of $\mathcal L$ nats per character is $\mathcal L/\ln 2$ bits per character. That's not just a unit change. By arithmetic coding, a model with cross-entropy $\mathcal L$ can compress held-out text to about $\mathcal L/\ln2$ bits per character (lesson 11: cross-entropy is the expected code length under the model). Prediction and compression are the same problem.

    For comparison, standard compressors on the whole Tiny Shakespeare file achieve: gzip 3.11 bits/char, xz 2.61, bzip2 2.36. Our run's validation loss of 1.53 nats is 2.20 bits/char, already better than bzip2, from a 0.8M-parameter model trained for about two minutes on a laptop CPU. The perplexity $e^{\mathcal L}$ is another common way to say it: $e^{1.6} \approx 4.95$, as uncertain as a uniform choice among about 5 characters (versus 65 at the start).

    Everything in the RL lessons (26–29) starts from a model trained exactly like this one, just much larger and on far more text: a pretrained next-token predictor. RLHF and DPO then adjust its distribution, with a KL penalty that keeps it close to this pretrained (or SFT) starting point.

    9. Problems

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    What loss should a freshly initialized model print on a vocabulary of 65 characters? And with a BPE vocabulary of 50,257?

    Solution

    $\ln 65 = 4.1744$ and $\ln 50{,}257 = 10.8249$. GPT-2 runs start near 10.8.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    A validation loss of 1.6 nats per character: how many bits per character, and what perplexity?

    Solution

    $1.6/\ln 2 = 2.308$ bits. Perplexity $e^{1.6} = 4.953$.

    Problem 3
    core

    Using the lesson-23 formula, compute the parameter count of a char-level GPT with $V = 65$, $T = 256$, $d = 384$, $L = 6$ and an untied head. (This is the "bigger" Shakespeare model often trained on a GPU.)

    Solution

    One block: $12\cdot384^2 + 13\cdot384 = 1{,}769{,}472 + 4{,}992 = 1{,}774{,}464$; six blocks: $10{,}646{,}784$. Embeddings: $65\cdot384 + 256\cdot384 = 24{,}960 + 98{,}304$. Final LN $768$, head $384\cdot65 = 24{,}960$. Total $10{,}795{,}776$, the "10.8M" model. At this size the blocks are 98.6% of the parameters.

    Problem 4
    core · derivation

    Show that if the initial logits are i.i.d. $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$, the expected initial loss is approximately $\ln V + \sigma^2/2$ for large $V$. Evaluate it for $V = 65$, $\sigma = 1$.

    Hint

    Write $\log\sum_j e^{z_j} = \log V + \log\big(\frac1V\sum_j e^{z_j}\big)$ and use the law of large numbers on the average.

    Solution

    $\mathcal L = \log\sum_j e^{z_j} - z_y = \log V + \log\big(\tfrac1V\sum_j e^{z_j}\big) - z_y$. For large $V$, the average $\tfrac1V\sum_j e^{z_j} \to \mathbb E[e^{z}] = e^{\sigma^2/2}$ (the moment generating function of a Gaussian: $\mathbb E[e^{tz}] = e^{t^2\sigma^2/2}$ at $t=1$). And $\mathbb E[z_y] = 0$. So $\mathbb E[\mathcal L] \approx \ln V + \sigma^2/2 = 4.1744 + 0.5 = 4.6744$. A simulation gives 4.666, so the approximation is excellent. It slightly overestimates for large $\sigma$, where a few huge $e^{z_j}$ dominate the sum and the law of large numbers kicks in slowly.

    Problem 5
    core

    Your step-0 loss is 0.9 and falls to 0.02 within 200 steps, but samples are garbage. Name the two most likely bugs, and a 1-line test for each.

    Solution

    A loss far below $\ln V$ before training means the model can see the answer. (1) Targets not shifted: $y = x$ instead of $y = x$ shifted by one, so the model just copies its input. Test: assert torch.equal(x[:, 1:], y[:, :-1]). (2) No causal mask: position $t$ attends to $t+1$, which is its own target. Test: change the input at a future position and assert the logits at earlier positions don't change (the causality test from workbooks 22–23). Garbage samples follow because at generation time the future isn't available to cheat with.

    Problem 6
    core · conceptual

    A colleague splits the data by drawing random windows and assigning each to train or validation with probability 0.9/0.1. Their validation loss is 1.2 while yours is 1.6 with the same model. Who has the better model?

    Solution

    Probably nobody. Their split leaks. A validation window overlaps heavily with training windows that start a few characters earlier or later, so their "validation" text was mostly trained on, and 1.2 measures memorization. With a contiguous split, validation is the final 10% of the file, text the model has never seen. Always split before windowing, along a boundary that matches how the model will be used.

    Problem 7
    stretch

    Our run processes about $1.2\times10^7$ training tokens with $8.3\times10^5$ parameters. Using $C \approx 6ND$ (lesson 25), estimate the training FLOPs. If your CPU sustains $5\times10^{10}$ FLOP/s on this workload, how many seconds would that take?

    Solution

    $C = 6 \cdot 826{,}368 \cdot 12{,}288{,}000 \approx 6.09\times10^{13}$ FLOPs. At $5\times10^{10}$ FLOP/s: about $1{,}218$ s. In practice the run is faster than this estimate on a multi-core laptop, and the formula ignores attention's $n^2$ term (sizeable here, since $T = 128$ is comparable to $d = 128$). The order of magnitude is the point: GPT-3's $3\times10^{23}$ is ten billion times more.

    10. Quick check

    One batch is $B = 32$ windows of length $T = 128$. How many next-token predictions contribute to the loss?
    The causal mask makes each position a separate example with its own prefix. The first position predicts from a context of one character, which is still a valid prediction.
    Your first printed loss is 4.17 for $V = 65$. What can you conclude?
    $\ln 65 = 4.17$ is what an untrained, near-uniform model scores. A leaking model would also start near 4.17. The leak shows up once training starts, as an implausibly fast drop.
    Train loss 1.45, validation loss 1.47, and both still decreasing at the end of the run. What should you try first?
    A tiny train/val gap with both curves still falling means capacity or training time is the bottleneck, not generalization (lesson 15).
