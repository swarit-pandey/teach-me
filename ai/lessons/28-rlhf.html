Part 7 · RL on transformers

RLHF: reward models & PPO with KL

A pretrained GPT can continue any text. It doesn't know which continuation a person would actually want. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) closes that gap in three moves. Collect human comparisons of model outputs, fit a reward model to them, then fine-tune the language model with PPO to score well under that reward while staying close to where it started. Each move is something you already know: maximum likelihood (lesson 10), KL divergence (lesson 11) and PPO (lesson 27).

By the end you can

  • Describe the pretrain → SFT → reward model → RL pipeline and what each stage contributes.
  • View text generation as a token-level MDP and say what the state, action, reward and policy are.
  • Derive the Bradley–Terry preference model and the reward-model loss as maximum likelihood.
  • Derive the closed-form optimum $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\,e^{r/\beta}$ of the KL-regularized objective, and explain what β trades off.
  • Explain reward hacking, and why a reverse KL penalty to the reference model is the standard defence.

    1. The pipeline

    InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022), the recipe behind ChatGPT, has four stages:

    StageDataObjectiveResult
    1. Pretraintrillions of web tokensnext-token cross-entropy (lesson 24)a model that can continue any text
    2. SFT~10⁴ prompts with human-written answerssame loss, on demonstrations (lesson 25)$\pi_{\text{SFT}}$: answers in the right format
    3. Reward model~10⁵ human comparisons "A is better than B"Bradley–Terry likelihood (§3)$r_\phi(x, y)$: a learned scalar judge
    4. RLprompts only; the policy writes its own answersmaximize $r_\phi$ − β·KL to $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$ (§5) with PPOthe aligned model

    Why not stop at SFT? Three reasons.

    • Judging is easier than writing. A labeller who couldn't write an ideal answer can still say which of two answers is better. Comparisons also turn out to be far more consistent across people than absolute 1–10 scores.
    • SFT only imitates. It learns to produce text that looks like the demonstrations, mistakes included, and gets no signal about what makes an answer good or bad. A reward lets the model learn from its own outputs, including outputs better than any demonstration.
    • Negative feedback. Cross-entropy on demonstrations only says "do this". Preferences also say "not that", which matters for things like refusing harmful requests or not making facts up.

    From now on, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ means the frozen SFT model. The RL stage starts with $\pi_\theta = \pi_{\text{ref}}$ and changes $\theta$.

    2. Generation as a token-level MDP

    Map lesson 26's vocabulary onto a chatbot answering prompt $x$:

    • State $s_t = (x, y_1, \dots, y_{t-1})$: the prompt plus the tokens written so far. It's Markov by construction, since it contains everything.
    • Action $a_t = y_t$, the next token, sampled from $\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)$, the transformer's softmax.
    • Transition: deterministic. Append the token. (No environment model to learn at all.)
    • Reward: zero until the end-of-sequence token, then the reward model's score $r_\phi(x, y)$. Usually $\gamma = 1$, because episodes are short and finite.

    The probability of a whole completion is the product of per-token probabilities, so its log is a sum:

    $$\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x, y_{\lt t}).$$

    Everything in lesson 27 now applies token by token. The "trajectory" is the completion. The policy gradient multiplies each token's score $\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)$ by an advantage, and the clipping happens per token. The sparse end-of-sequence reward is the credit-assignment problem in its purest form. Which of 200 tokens made the answer good? A learned critic plus GAE spreads the credit back.

    Try it: build a completion one token at a time. Click a bar to choose the next token, or press sample. Grey bars are the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$; blue bars are an RL-tuned policy $\pi_\theta$ that has learned to like the letter a. The bottom strip shows the RLHF reward each token earns. Every token pays a KL penalty $-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ (pink if negative). Only the last token also receives the reward model's score (here: labellers like exactly three a's). Pick unlikely-under-the-reference tokens and watch the penalty grow.

    3. Preferences: the Bradley–Terry model

    Labellers give us pairs: for prompt $x$, completion $y_w$ ("winner") was preferred over $y_l$ ("loser"). We want a scalar reward $r(x,y)$ that explains those choices. The Bradley–Terry model (1952, originally for ranking chess players and sports teams) says each item has a latent strength, and the probability that one beats another is a softmax over the two:

    $$P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{e^{r(x,y_w)}}{e^{r(x,y_w)} + e^{r(x,y_l)}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l))}} = \sigma\big(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l)\big).$$

    (Divide top and bottom by $e^{r_w}$ to get the middle form.) It's logistic regression (lesson 14) on the difference of two scores. Two consequences follow immediately:

    • Only differences matter. Adding any constant $c(x)$ to every reward for the same prompt changes nothing, so the reward scale and offset are not identifiable from preferences. RLHF never needs them to be (§6 shows the optimal policy is also unchanged).
    • A gap of 1 in reward means about a 73% win rate ($\sigma(1) = 0.731$). A gap of 3 means 95%.

    Maximum likelihood → the reward-model loss

    Treat the comparisons as independent Bernoulli observations and maximize their likelihood (lesson 10). With a parameterized reward $r_\phi$, the negative log-likelihood of the dataset $\mathcal D$ is

    $$\boxed{\;\mathcal L(\phi) = -\,\mathbb E_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big]\;}$$

    Its gradient with respect to the margin $\Delta = r_w - r_l$ is

    $$\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta}\big[-\log\sigma(\Delta)\big] = -(1 - \sigma(\Delta)) = -\sigma(-\Delta).$$

    Pairs the model already ranks confidently ($\Delta \gg 0$) contribute almost nothing. Pairs it gets wrong ($\Delta \lt 0$) contribute nearly the full unit push. It's the same "learn from your mistakes" weighting as cross-entropy.

    Try it: left, drag the dot along the margin axis $\Delta = r_w - r_l$. You see the win probability $\sigma(\Delta)$ (blue), the loss $-\log\sigma(\Delta)$ (orange) and its gradient. Right, five responses A–E with hidden true rewards (hollow circles) generate $N$ random noisy comparisons. Press fit to run gradient descent on the Bradley–Terry loss (filled circles). With few comparisons the fit is rough. With many it lands on the true values, up to a shift: we pin the mean to zero, because preferences can't tell.

    4. Training the reward model

    The reward model is usually the SFT transformer itself with the vocabulary head replaced by a scalar head, a linear layer applied to the final hidden state of the last token. Causal attention means only the last position has seen the whole prompt and response. Training data come from showing labellers $K$ completions per prompt and asking for a ranking. Each ranking yields $\binom K2$ pairs, all used in the loss above.

    Two facts keep expectations realistic:

    • Labels are noisy. Human labellers agree with each other only about 63–77% of the time in published datasets. Even a perfect reward model can't beat the labels' own consistency. Under Bradley–Terry, a pair with true gap $\Delta$ is labelled "correctly" only $\sigma(|\Delta|)$ of the time. That sets the Bayes limit on held-out accuracy (problem 9). InstructGPT's 6B reward model reached about 72%.
    • The RM is only trustworthy on the distribution it was trained on. It saw SFT-like answers. Once RL pushes the policy toward unusual text, the RM's scores there are extrapolations. That's the root of reward hacking (§7).
    Worked example: one reward-model gradient

    The RM scores the chosen answer $r_w = 1.5$ and the rejected one $r_l = 0.5$. Then $P(y_w\succ y_l) = \sigma(1) = 0.731$ and the loss is $-\log 0.731 = 0.313$.

    The gradient of the loss with respect to $r_w$ is $-(1-0.731) = -0.269$, and with respect to $r_l$ it is $+0.269$. Gradient descent raises $r_w$ and lowers $r_l$ by equal amounts, so the margin widens. Backprop then carries $\pm0.269$ into the transformer through each completion's last-token hidden state.

    5. The objective: reward minus KL

    Now fine-tune the policy to get high reward. The objective is not just $\mathbb E[r_\phi]$. It is

    $$\boxed{\;\max_\theta\ \ \mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D,\ y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big] \;-\; \beta\,\mathbb E_x\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big]\;}$$

    Why penalize moving away from the SFT model?

    1. The reward model is wrong off-distribution. An optimizer will find and exploit its errors (§7). Staying close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ keeps the policy where the RM's judgements were learned.
    2. Keep what pretraining bought. Fluency, knowledge and diversity live in $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Unconstrained reward maximization collapses onto a few high-scoring strings.
    3. Optimization stability. It's a soft trust region over the whole run, complementing PPO's per-batch clipping.

    Which KL, and why it's the reverse one

    The penalty is $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\big]$, the expectation under the policy being trained. In lesson 11's terms this is the reverse, mode-seeking KL. It is infinite if $\pi_\theta$ puts mass anywhere $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ gives (nearly) zero probability, so the policy can't invent text the SFT model would never write. But it is happy for $\pi_\theta$ to drop some of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$'s modes and concentrate on the good ones. That's exactly the behaviour we want: become pickier, never weirder. It's also the only direction we can estimate cheaply. We sample $y$ from $\pi_\theta$ anyway, and the estimate is just $\log\pi_\theta(y) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)$, a difference of two forward passes.

    Because $\log\pi(y\mid x)$ is a sum over tokens, the sequence KL splits into per-token terms (the chain rule for KL, problem 7). So RLHF implementations fold the penalty into a per-token reward:

    $$r_t = -\beta\,\Big(\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid s_t)\Big) \;+\; \mathbb 1[t = T]\; r_\phi(x, y).$$

    That's what the token figure in §2 displays, and it is the reward the PPO critic and GAE see.

    6. The optimal policy, in closed form

    For a single prompt, forget neural networks and ask which distribution $\pi$ over completions maximizes the objective. Write it out and pull out $-\beta$:

    $$\sum_y \pi(y)\, r(y) - \beta\sum_y \pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} = -\beta\sum_y\pi(y)\Big[\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} - \frac{r(y)}{\beta}\Big] = -\beta\sum_y\pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\,e^{r(y)/\beta}}.$$

    The denominator isn't a distribution yet. Normalize it with $Z = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}$ and define $\pi^*(y) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}/Z$. Substituting $\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta} = Z\pi^*$:

    $$= -\beta\sum_y\pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi^*(y)} + \beta\log Z = -\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi^*) + \beta\log Z.$$

    $\beta\log Z$ doesn't depend on $\pi$, and KL ≥ 0 with equality only at $\pi = \pi^*$ (lesson 11). So the unique maximizer is

    $$\boxed{\;\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big)\;}$$

    and the optimal value is $\beta\log Z$. This is the single most important equation for the rest of Part 7. Read it as reweighting the reference model by exponentiated reward. β is a temperature: as β → ∞, $\pi^* \to \pi_{\text{ref}}$ (ignore the reward); as β → 0, all mass goes to $\arg\max_y r$ (ignore the reference). Adding $c(x)$ to every reward multiplies numerator and $Z$ by $e^{c/\beta}$ and changes nothing, consistent with Bradley–Terry not identifying that constant.

    Try it: next-token choice after the prompt "The capital of France is". Grey bars are $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, the number under each token is its reward, and blue bars are $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}$. Slide β down from large to small and watch mass flow toward " Paris". Right, every β gives one point on the trade-off curve between expected reward and KL from the reference. No policy can sit above this curve (it's the exact optimum). Notice the diminishing returns: the last bit of reward costs a lot of KL.
    Worked example: computing π*

    Three completions with $\pi_{\text{ref}} = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ and rewards $r = (0, 1, 2)$, at β = 1.

    Unnormalized weights $\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r}$: $0.5$, $0.3e = 0.8155$, $0.2e^2 = 1.4778$. They sum to $Z = 2.7933$. So $\pi^* = (0.179, 0.292, 0.529)$.

    Expected reward rises from $0.7$ to $1.350$. $\mathrm{KL}(\pi^*\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = 0.323$ nats. Objective: $1.350 - 0.323 = 1.027 = \log Z$ ✓ ($\beta\log Z$ with β = 1).

    7. Reward hacking

    Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The reward model is a proxy for what humans want. It is accurate where it has data and has arbitrary errors elsewhere. Optimizing hard against a proxy finds exactly those errors. Documented examples include answers that get longer and longer (labellers mildly preferred detail, the RM learned "longer = better"), excessive flattery and hedging, confident-sounding wrong answers, and, for code models, special-casing the unit tests.

    In the toy below, the reward model is accurate on common answers but overrates some rare styles: flattery, all-caps enthusiasm, keyword stuffing. Those got few labels, so their RM errors are large. We compute the exact optimal policy $\pi^*_\beta$ against the proxy for every β and then evaluate it with the true reward.

    Try it: left, as optimization pressure increases (β decreases, KL from the reference grows to the right), the proxy reward the optimizer sees rises monotonically. The true reward first rises, then falls below where it started. Right, the policy at the current β (blue) versus the reference (grey), with each response's true and proxy reward. Slide β to the peak of the true curve, about 0.7–1, then keep going and watch the mass move onto "keyword-stuffed". The KL penalty is what keeps a real system near the peak.

    The KL penalty limits hacking, but it doesn't prevent it. It only limits how far the policy may travel. Practitioners also retrain the reward model on fresh comparisons of the current policy's outputs ("iterated RLHF"), use ensembles of reward models, early-stop on held-out human evaluations, and cap response length. Gao et al. (2023) measured this exact rise-then-fall curve on real models, with gold reward against $\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}$.

    8. PPO for RLHF, concretely

    Put lesson 27's PPO on the token MDP. Four networks are involved: the policy $\pi_\theta$ (trained), the frozen reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, the frozen reward model $r_\phi$, and the critic $V_\psi$ (trained; usually initialized from the reward model). One iteration:

    1. Rollout. Sample a batch of prompts and generate completions with $\pi_\theta$. Store each token's $\log\pi_\theta$ (these become $\log\pi_{\text{old}}$), $\log\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and $V_\psi(s_t)$, plus the RM score of each finished completion.
    2. Rewards. Per-token KL penalties, plus the score on the last token (§5).
    3. Advantages. GAE over each completion's tokens with γ = 1 and λ ≈ 0.95, then whiten them.
    4. Update. A few epochs of the clipped PPO loss for $\pi_\theta$ and squared error for $V_\psi$ on the same batch.

    Typical practical additions are an adaptive β (raise β when the measured KL exceeds a target, lower it when below; InstructGPT used a fixed β ≈ 0.02), mixing some pretraining gradient back in ("PPO-ptx") to avoid forgetting, and normalizing the reward model's scores.

    The workbook does all of this at toy scale. A 2-layer TinyGPT is pretrained on a synthetic "language" over the letters a–f (the SFT model). A transformer reward model is fit to 3000 Bradley–Terry-sampled preferences from labellers who like exactly three a's. Then PPO with per-token KL penalties fine-tunes the policy. The test checks that the reward rises by more than one point while the KL stays bounded. train_rlhf.py runs the full pipeline against the learned reward model at a large and a small β, and prints the RM score next to the labellers' true score. Small β is where you can catch reward hacking in the act.

    What the real run shows (train_rlhf.py on a laptop CPU, about 12 s):

    • The reward model reaches 70.7% held-out preference accuracy. The best achievable is 70.5%, because the labellers themselves are noisy. Yet it has a blind spot: it scores sequences with 4–8 a's at about +2.2, as high as exactly three. The labellers' true score falls to −2 at 8 a's. The reward model only ever saw pairs from the SFT model, which rarely produces many a's, so it never learned the downside.
    • β = 0.3: the true score rises from 1.45 to 2.38 and the KL stays around 1.
    • β = 0.05: the reward-model score climbs higher and the KL triples to about 3.5, but the true score ends at 2.31, no better. Samples start to fill up with a's (caaefaaf, fabaabae). The policy is spending its KL budget pushing into the reward model's blind spot. That is reward hacking caught early, and it's why β is the most important knob in RLHF.

    9. Problems

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    A reward model scores two answers $r_w = 2.3$ and $r_l = 0.3$. Under Bradley–Terry, what's the probability a labeller prefers the first?

    Solution

    $\sigma(2.3-0.3) = \sigma(2) = 1/(1+e^{-2}) = 0.8808$. Only the gap matters: $(102.3, 100.3)$ would give the same answer.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    A freshly initialized reward model outputs 0 for everything. What is its Bradley–Terry loss on any dataset?

    Solution

    $-\log\sigma(0) = \log 2 \approx 0.693$, the loss of a coin flip. It's a useful sanity check: your RM training curve should start at 0.693 and go down.

    Problem 3
    core

    Two next tokens with $\pi_{\text{ref}} = (0.5, 0.5)$ and rewards $(0, 1)$. With β = 0.5, what is $\pi^*$ of the second token? What is the optimal objective value $\beta\log Z$?

    Solution

    Weights $0.5e^0 = 0.5$ and $0.5e^{1/0.5} = 0.5e^2 = 3.6945$. So $\pi^*_2 = e^2/(1+e^2) = 0.8808$. $Z = 4.1945$ and $\beta\log Z = 0.5\cdot1.4338 = 0.7169$. Check: $\mathbb E[r] = 0.8808$, KL $= 0.8808\ln(1.7616) + 0.1192\ln(0.2384) = 0.4987 - 0.1709 = 0.3278$, and $0.8808 - 0.5\cdot0.3278 = 0.7169$ ✓.

    Problem 4
    core · proof

    Re-derive $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}$ with a Lagrange multiplier instead of "completing the KL". Maximize $\sum_y\pi(y)r(y) - \beta\sum_y\pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$ subject to $\sum_y\pi(y) = 1$.

    Hint

    Differentiate with respect to a single $\pi(y)$. $\frac{d}{dp}\,p\log\frac{p}{q} = \log\frac pq + 1$.

    Solution

    Stationarity: $r(y) - \beta\big(\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} + 1\big) - \lambda = 0$, so $\log\pi(y) = \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y) + r(y)/\beta - 1 - \lambda/\beta$, i.e. $\pi(y) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}\cdot e^{-1-\lambda/\beta}$. The constant is fixed by normalization: $e^{-1-\lambda/\beta} = 1/Z$. The objective is strictly concave in $\pi$ (negative entropy is strictly convex), so this stationary point is the maximum.

    Problem 5
    core

    A 3-token completion has policy log-probs $(-0.2, -1.0, -0.5)$, reference log-probs $(-0.4, -1.5, -0.5)$, RM score 2, and β = 0.1. Compute the per-token RLHF rewards and their sum.

    Solution

    Log-ratios: $0.2, 0.5, 0$. Rewards: $-0.02, -0.05, 0 + 2 = 2.0$. Sum $= 1.93 = 2 - 0.1\cdot0.7$, where $0.7$ is this sample's KL estimate. Note the first two tokens get negative reward for being more likely under the policy than under the reference.

    Problem 6
    core · concept

    Show that adding a prompt-dependent constant $c(x)$ to the reward changes neither the Bradley–Terry likelihood nor $\pi^*$. Then explain why reward models are still often normalized (e.g. to mean 0 over SFT samples) before PPO.

    Solution

    BT depends on $r_w - r_l$, in which $c(x)$ cancels. $\pi^* = \pi_{\text{ref}}e^{(r+c)/\beta}/\sum\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{(r+c)/\beta}$: the factor $e^{c/\beta}$ cancels. So the optimum is invariant. PPO, however, learns from finite samples with a critic and advantage estimates. A large constant offset makes the critic's job harder and the early advantages badly scaled (the lesson 27 variance argument). Normalizing is about optimization, not the objective.

    Problem 7
    core · proof

    Prove the chain rule for sequence KL: $\mathrm{KL}(\pi(y)\|\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)) = \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}\big[\sum_t \log\frac{\pi(y_t\mid y_{\lt t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid y_{\lt t})}\big] = \sum_t\mathbb E_{y_{\lt t}\sim\pi}\big[\mathrm{KL}(\pi(\cdot\mid y_{\lt t})\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid y_{\lt t}))\big]$.

    Solution

    $\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} = \sum_t\log\frac{\pi(y_t\mid y_{\lt t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid y_{\lt t})}$ because both are products of conditionals. Take $\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}$ and use linearity. For the $t$-th term, condition on the prefix: $\mathbb E_{y_{\lt t}}\mathbb E_{y_t\mid y_{\lt t}}[\log\frac{\pi(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid\cdot)}]$, and the inner expectation is the per-token KL. That's why a per-token penalty $-\beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ summed over the completion is an unbiased sample of the sequence KL.

    Problem 8
    core · concept

    Suppose RLHF used the forward KL $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi_\theta)$ instead. What behaviour would it encourage, and why is it awkward to estimate during PPO?

    Solution

    Forward KL is mass-covering (lesson 11). It blows up wherever $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ has mass but $\pi_\theta$ doesn't, so the policy must keep putting probability on everything the SFT model might say, including the mediocre answers we wanted to drop. It's the SFT cross-entropy objective in disguise. Estimating it needs samples from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (an extra generation pass per batch), whereas the reverse KL uses the policy samples PPO already has.

    Problem 9
    stretch

    Labels are sampled from Bradley–Terry with the true reward. Every test pair has a true reward gap of exactly 1. What's the best held-out accuracy any reward model can achieve?

    Solution

    The best you can do is always predict the truly better answer. That matches the label with probability $\sigma(1) = 0.731$. In general the Bayes accuracy is $\mathbb E[\sigma(|\Delta|)]$. That's why 70%-ish RM accuracy on human data is not a sign of a broken model. The workbook's test compares your RM to exactly this limit.

    Problem 10
    stretch

    Using the hacking toy's numbers, at β = 1 the optimal policy has KL ≈ 0.26 and true reward 0.44 (from 0.20 at the reference). At β = 0.2: KL ≈ 1.97, true reward −0.35. Suppose a training run tracks only the RM score. Propose two signals you'd monitor to catch the problem, and explain why each works.

    Solution

    (1) KL to the reference: hacking needs the policy to move far, so a KL budget (or adaptive β targeting one) stops before the steep part of the curve. (2) A held-out "gold" evaluation: periodic human or stronger-model judgements, or a second reward model trained on different data. When proxy and gold diverge, you've crossed the peak. Other good answers: response-length statistics, sample diversity or entropy, and reading samples.

    10. Quick check

    In the token-level MDP for an LLM, what is the transition function?
    All the randomness is in the policy's sampling. That's why "model-free vs model-based" barely matters here, and why the reward and KL are the only interesting parts of the environment.
    Why is the reward model trained on comparisons rather than asking labellers for a 1–10 score?
    Different people use 1–10 scales differently; "which is better?" is easier and more reliable. The lost offset is harmless: §6 showed $\pi^*$ is invariant to it.
    β → 0 in the KL-regularized objective. What does the optimal policy become?
    $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}$: as β shrinks, the exponential swamps $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and only the argmax survives. That's where reward hacking lives.
    The KL penalty uses $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$. What does this direction forbid?
    Reverse KL $\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta}[\log\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}}]$ explodes where $\pi_{\text{ref}}\approx0$ but $\pi_\theta$ isn't. It is mode-seeking: dropping modes is cheap, and inventing new ones is expensive.
