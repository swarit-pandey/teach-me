Part 1 · Linear algebra

Vectors & the dot product

Every piece of data a model touches — an image, a word, a user — becomes a list of numbers. Treat that list as an arrow in space and you get geometry: lengths, angles, similarity. The dot product is the single most-used operation in all of deep learning.

By the end you can

  • Move fluently between the three views of a vector: arrow, list of numbers, point in data space.
  • Add, scale and combine vectors, and say what the span of a set of vectors is.
  • Compute norms, dot products, angles and projections — and explain each geometrically.
  • Explain why cosine similarity is how embeddings are compared (and why attention is built on dot products).

    1. What is a vector?

    A vector is an ordered list of numbers. In two dimensions, $\mathbf{v} = (3, 2)$ — or written as a column,

    $$\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

    There are three equally valid ways to picture it, and fluency means switching between them without effort:

    ViewPictureWhere you'll meet it
    ArrowGo 3 right, 2 up. Only direction and length matter — you can slide it anywhere.Gradients, forces, directions in embedding space
    List of numbers[3, 2] — an array in memory.Every tensor in every model
    PointThe tip of the arrow when its tail is at the origin.A data point: one house, one image, one word

    The power of the idea is that it doesn't stop at 2 or 3 dimensions. A 28×28 grayscale image is a vector with 784 numbers. A word in GPT-2 is a vector with 768 numbers. You can't draw 768-dimensional arrows, but every formula in this lesson works unchanged in any number of dimensions. We build intuition in 2-D and trust the algebra for the rest.

    Notation: we write vectors in bold ($\mathbf{v}$), their components with subscripts ($v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$), and say $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ ("v is in R-n") when it has $n$ real components.

    2. Adding and scaling

    Two operations define everything else. Addition is component-wise; geometrically it's "tip-to-tail": walk along $\mathbf{u}$, then along $\mathbf{v}$.

    $$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 + v_1 \\ u_2 + v_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

    Scalar multiplication stretches (or flips, if negative) a vector: $c\,\mathbf{v} = (c v_1, c v_2)$.

    Try it: drag the dots at the arrow tips to move u and v. The faint parallelogram shows that $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}$: either path lands on the same tip. Use the slider to scale $\mathbf{v}$ by $c$ — try a negative $c$.

    3. Linear combinations and span

    Combine both operations and you get a linear combination:

    $$a\,\mathbf{u} + b\,\mathbf{v}, \qquad a, b \in \mathbb{R}.$$

    The set of every vector you can reach this way is the span of $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$. In 2-D, two vectors that don't point along the same line span the entire plane — you can reach any point. If they are collinear (one is a multiple of the other), you're stuck on a line: the second vector added nothing new. We say they are linearly dependent.

    Try it: move $a$ and $b$ to reach different points. Tick "show span" to light up many combinations. Then drag $\mathbf{v}$ until it lines up with $\mathbf{u}$ and watch the span collapse to a line.

    A neural network layer outputs linear combinations of learned vectors, followed by a non-linearity. Whether the vectors a model learns are independent or redundant is a question about span. In lesson 04 you'll measure this with rank and the SVD.

    4. Length: the norm

    By Pythagoras, the length of $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2)$ is $\sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}$. In $n$ dimensions the same formula holds and is called the Euclidean or L2 norm:

    $$\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i v_i^2}.$$

    A vector with norm 1 is a unit vector. Any non-zero vector can be normalized: $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{v} / \|\mathbf{v}\|$ keeps the direction and throws away the length.

    There are other ways to measure size. The L1 norm $\|\mathbf{v}\|_1 = \sum_i |v_i|$ is "taxicab" distance, and the L∞ norm is $\max_i |v_i|$. The cleanest way to see the difference is to draw every vector of norm exactly 1, the "unit ball", under each norm:

    Unit balls for L1 (diamond), L2 (circle), and L∞ (square). Slide $p$ to see the general $L^p$ norm $\big(\sum_i |v_i|^p\big)^{1/p}$ morph between them. You'll meet L1 and L2 again as regularizers in lesson 15. L1's sharp corners on the axes are why it produces sparse weights.

    5. The dot product

    The dot product of two vectors multiplies matching components and adds up the results:

    $$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \cdots + a_n b_n = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i.$$

    It takes two vectors and returns one number. Algebraically that's all it is. The surprise is what the number means geometrically:

    $$\boxed{\;\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta\;}$$

    where $\theta$ is the angle between them. So the dot product measures how much two vectors point the same way, scaled by their lengths:

    • $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} > 0$: angle under 90°, broadly the same direction.
    • $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0$: exactly perpendicular (orthogonal).
    • $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} < 0$: angle over 90°, pointing apart.
    Try it: drag $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. The shaded arc shows $\theta$. Its color tracks the sign of the dot product (blue positive, pink negative). The dashed segment is the projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $\mathbf{a}$ (section 6). Check that both formulas always agree.

    Why do the two formulas agree?

    The proof is short and worth owning. Apply the law of cosines to the triangle with sides $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}$:

    $$\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{b}\|^2 - 2\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta.$$

    Now expand the left side algebraically, using $\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 = \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}$ and the fact that the dot product distributes like ordinary multiplication:

    $$\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|^2 = (\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b})\cdot(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}) = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} + \|\mathbf{b}\|^2.$$

    Set the two expressions equal. Everything cancels except $-2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = -2\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$. $\blacksquare$

    Since $|\cos\theta| \le 1$, we get the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for free: $|\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}| \le \|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|$, with equality exactly when the vectors are parallel. It's the reason cosine similarity always lies in $[-1, 1]$.

    Worked example

    Let $\mathbf{a} = (3, 4)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (4, -3)$. Then $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 3\cdot4 + 4\cdot(-3) = 0$, so they are perpendicular, even though neither lies on an axis.

    Now $\mathbf{c} = (1, 1)$: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c} = 7$, $\|\mathbf{a}\| = 5$, $\|\mathbf{c}\| = \sqrt2$, so $\cos\theta = 7/(5\sqrt2) \approx 0.990$, giving $\theta \approx 8.1°$. Nearly the same direction.

    6. Projection: the shadow of one vector on another

    How much of $\mathbf{b}$ points along $\mathbf{a}$? Shine a light perpendicular to $\mathbf{a}$ and look at $\mathbf{b}$'s shadow. The shadow's signed length is the scalar projection $\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|}$. Point that length along $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ and you get the vector projection:

    $$\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\;\mathbf{a}.$$

    The leftover $\mathbf{b} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{a}$ (problem 4 asks you to prove it). This decomposes $\mathbf{b}$ into a part along $\mathbf{a}$ and a part orthogonal to it. That decomposition is the core of least-squares regression (lesson 13) and of the Gram–Schmidt process.

    7. Cosine similarity: comparing meanings

    If you only care about direction and not length, normalize both vectors first. That gives cosine similarity:

    $$\cos(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|} \in [-1, 1].$$

    Language models map words (and sentences, images and code) to embedding vectors, arranged so that related meanings point in similar directions. Search engines, recommendation systems and retrieval-augmented generation all rank results by cosine similarity. Below is a toy 2-D "embedding space". Real ones have hundreds of dimensions, but the arithmetic is identical.

    Left: word vectors (drag any of them). Click a word to make it the query; the bars on its arrow show its cosine similarity to every other word. Right: the full matrix of pairwise cosine similarities. Notice the matrix is symmetric and its diagonal is all 1s. Why? (Problem 6.)

    A transformer's attention (lesson 22) works the same way. Each token emits a query vector, and every token emits a key vector. The model computes the dot product of the query with every key, and a large dot product means "pay attention here". You've just learned the core operation of GPT.

    8. Into high dimensions

    Everything above works in $\mathbb{R}^n$. One fact here surprises everyone: in high dimensions, random vectors are almost always nearly orthogonal. If you draw two random directions in $\mathbb{R}^{768}$, their cosine similarity is almost certainly within about $\pm 0.1$ of zero. Slide $n$ and watch the distribution of cosine similarities between random pairs squeeze toward 0:

    Histogram of cosine similarity between 2000 pairs of random Gaussian vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Its spread shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$, which is why a high-dimensional space has room for a huge number of almost-independent "concept directions".

    9. Problems

    Do these with pencil and paper before opening the solutions. The struggle is where the learning happens.

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    Let $\mathbf{u} = (2, -1, 3)$ and $\mathbf{v} = (1, 4, 0)$. Compute $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$, $3\mathbf{u} - 2\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$, and $\|\mathbf{u}\|$.

    Enter $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$:

    Solution

    $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v} = (3, 3, 3)$. $3\mathbf{u}-2\mathbf{v} = (6,-3,9)-(2,8,0) = (4,-11,9)$. $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 2 - 4 + 0 = -2$. $\|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{4+1+9} = \sqrt{14}\approx 3.742$.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    Find the angle (in degrees) between $\mathbf{a} = (1, 0)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (1, \sqrt3)$.

    Solution

    $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 1$, $\|\mathbf{a}\|=1$, $\|\mathbf{b}\| = \sqrt{1+3} = 2$. $\cos\theta = 1/2$, so $\theta = 60°$.

    Problem 3
    core

    For what value of $k$ are $(k, 2, -1)$ and $(3, k, 4)$ orthogonal?

    Hint

    Orthogonal means the dot product is zero. Write it out; it's linear in $k$.

    Solution

    $3k + 2k - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow 5k = 4 \Rightarrow k = 0.8$.

    Problem 4
    core · proof

    Show that $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{a}$.

    Solution

    $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} - \dfrac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\,(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0.$ The coefficient $\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}$ is exactly the one that makes the residual orthogonal. Equivalently, it's the $c$ that minimizes $\|\mathbf{b} - c\mathbf{a}\|^2$. Try deriving it that way too by expanding and setting the derivative in $c$ to zero.

    Problem 5
    core · proof

    (a) Prove $\|\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 + 2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} + \|\mathbf{b}\|^2$. (b) Use it and Cauchy–Schwarz to prove the triangle inequality $\|\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\| \le \|\mathbf{a}\| + \|\mathbf{b}\|$. Draw the picture that makes this obvious.

    Solution

    (a) $(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})\cdot(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} + \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{b}$, and the dot product is symmetric.

    (b) $\|\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 + 2\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} + \|\mathbf{b}\|^2 \le \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 + 2\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\| + \|\mathbf{b}\|^2 = (\|\mathbf{a}\|+\|\mathbf{b}\|)^2$. Take square roots, which preserves order for non-negative numbers. Picture: one side of a triangle is never longer than the other two combined.

    Problem 6
    core

    Let $E$ be the $n\times n$ matrix of pairwise cosine similarities between $n$ word vectors. Explain why (a) $E$ is symmetric, (b) its diagonal is all 1s, and (c) if every vector is first normalized to unit length and stacked as the rows of a matrix $W$, then $E = W W^\top$. (Part (c) uses matrix products, which lesson 03 covers. Come back to it then if it's unclear now.)

    Solution

    (a) $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{a}$. (b) $\cos(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}) = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2/\|\mathbf{a}\|^2 = 1$. (c) Entry $(i,j)$ of $WW^\top$ is row $i$ of $W$ dotted with column $j$ of $W^\top$, which is row $j$ of $W$: that's $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i\cdot\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j$. Computing all similarities is a single matrix multiply, and that's exactly how attention scores are computed in bulk.

    Problem 7
    stretch

    The vectors $(1,1,0)$, $(1,-1,0)$, $(0,0,1)$ are mutually orthogonal. Write $\mathbf{x} = (3, 1, 2)$ as a linear combination of them without solving a system of equations. Use projections.

    Hint

    For an orthogonal basis, the coefficient on $\mathbf{q}_i$ is $\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{q}_i / \mathbf{q}_i\cdot\mathbf{q}_i$. Enter the three coefficients separated by commas.

    Solution

    $c_1 = (3+1)/2 = 2$, $c_2 = (3-1)/2 = 1$, $c_3 = 2/1 = 2$. Check: $2(1,1,0) + 1(1,-1,0) + 2(0,0,1) = (3,1,2)$. ✓ Orthogonal bases make coordinates trivial, which is why so much of numerical linear algebra tries to find one.

    10. Quick check

    Two embedding vectors have a large dot product but a cosine similarity of 0.05. What's going on?
    $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$. Cosine strips the lengths away and leaves only direction. That's why retrieval systems usually normalize embeddings first.
    In $\mathbb{R}^3$, what is the span of $(1,2,3)$ and $(2,4,6)$?
    The second vector is $2\times$ the first, so all their combinations are multiples of $(1,2,3)$. That's a line.
    You double the length of $\mathbf{a}$. What happens to $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}$?
    $\frac{(2\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{b}}{(2\mathbf{a})\cdot(2\mathbf{a})}(2\mathbf{a}) = \frac{4\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{4\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{a}$. The shadow on a line doesn't care how long the arrow defining the line is.

    11. Summary

    ConceptFormulaMeaning
    Norm$\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\sum v_i^2}$length
    Dot product$\sum a_i b_i = \|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|\cos\theta$alignment × lengths
    Cosine similarity$\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|\|\mathbf{b}\|}$alignment only, in $[-1,1]$
    Projection$\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{a}$the part of $\mathbf{b}$ along $\mathbf{a}$
    Span$\{a\mathbf{u} + b\mathbf{v}\}$everything reachable by combining
