Part 1 · Linear algebra

Matmul, systems, inverse & rank

Matrix multiplication looks like a fiddly rule for combining grids of numbers. It is really one idea, doing one transformation after another, and once you see it that way the rest of this lesson follows: solving equations, undoing a transformation, and measuring how much information a matrix destroys.

By the end you can

  • Explain why $AB$ means "apply $B$, then $A$", why the order matters, and which shapes can be multiplied.
  • Compute a matrix product four different ways and say what each view is good for.
  • Read $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ as intersecting lines (row picture) and as mixing columns (column picture), and solve it by Gaussian elimination.
  • Say when an inverse exists, compute one, and explain what a singular matrix does to space.
  • Find the rank, column space and null space of a matrix, and use rank–nullity.

    1. Matrix multiplication is composition

    Lesson 02 showed that a $2\times2$ matrix is a transformation of the plane, and its columns record where the basis vectors $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$ land. Now apply two transformations in a row: first $B$, then $A$. A vector $\mathbf{x}$ goes to $B\mathbf{x}$, then to $A(B\mathbf{x})$.

    The combined map is still linear, so it is also a matrix. Call it $AB$. Which matrix is it? Follow the basis vectors. $\hat\imath$ first lands on $B$'s first column $\mathbf{b}_1$; then $A$ sends that to $A\mathbf{b}_1$. Same for $\hat\jmath$. So

    $$AB = \begin{bmatrix} | & | \\ A\mathbf{b}_1 & A\mathbf{b}_2 \\ | & | \end{bmatrix}, \qquad (AB)\mathbf{x} = A(B\mathbf{x}).$$

    That's the whole definition. Every formula for matrix multiplication is a way of computing "column $j$ of $AB$ is $A$ times column $j$ of $B$".

    Read right to left. $AB\mathbf{x}$ applies $B$ first, because $B$ is the one touching $\mathbf{x}$. It's the same as function composition $f(g(x))$: written left to right, executed right to left.

    Since "rotate then shear" and "shear then rotate" are different motions, there's no reason to expect $AB = BA$. Usually it isn't.

    Try it: left panel is $AB$ (do $B$ first), right panel is $BA$ (do $A$ first). Drag the slider $t$ from 0 to 2 to watch the first transformation happen, then the second. Pick "rotate 90°" and "shear" and compare where the F ends up. Then try two rotations, or two scalings: some pairs do commute. Which ones, and why?
    Worked example: order matters

    Let $R = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ (rotate 90° counter-clockwise) and $S = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ (horizontal shear).

    $RS$ (shear first): the columns of $S$ are $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Rotating them gives $R(1,0) = (0,1)$ and $R(1,1) = (-1,1)$. So $RS = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$.

    $SR$ (rotate first): the columns of $R$ are $(0,1)$ and $(-1,0)$. Shearing gives $S(0,1) = (1,1)$ and $S(-1,0) = (-1,0)$. So $SR = \begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$.

    Different matrices, different pictures. Both have determinant 1, though: $\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B$ in either order, because area scale factors just multiply (lesson 02).

    A neural network is a chain of layers $\mathbf{x} \mapsto W_3\,\sigma(W_2\,\sigma(W_1\mathbf{x}))$. Take the non-linearities $\sigma$ away and the whole stack collapses to a single matrix $W_3W_2W_1$: depth buys nothing. That's why the non-linearity is essential (lesson 16).

    2. Shapes: what can be multiplied

    Matrices don't have to be square. An $m\times n$ matrix ($m$ rows, $n$ columns) eats vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and outputs vectors in $\mathbb{R}^m$: each of its $n$ columns is an $m$-dimensional vector, and $A\mathbf{x}$ mixes them.

    For $AB$ to make sense, $A$ must accept what $B$ outputs. So:

    $$\underbrace{A}_{m\times n}\;\underbrace{B}_{n\times p} = \underbrace{AB}_{m\times p}.$$

    The inner dimensions must match and cancel; the outer ones survive. Entry $(i,j)$ of the result is

    $$(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} A_{ik}B_{kj}.$$

    Computing it naively costs $m\cdot n\cdot p$ multiply-adds. Two more facts you'll use constantly:

    • Associative: $(AB)C = A(BC)$. Composition doesn't care how you bracket it. But the cost does (problem 3).
    • Transpose reverses order: $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$. Check the shapes: $(m\times p)^\top = p\times m = (p\times n)(n\times m)$.

    In code, a batch of $N$ inputs sits in the rows of $X$ ($N\times d_\text{in}$). A linear layer with weights $W$ ($d_\text{out}\times d_\text{in}$) computes all outputs at once as $XW^\top$ ($N\times d_\text{out}$). PyTorch's nn.Linear does exactly this (lesson 19). Most shape errors you'll ever hit are a violated inner-dimension rule.

    3. Four ways to see one product

    The same sum $\sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj}$ can be grouped four ways. Each is the right mental model in a different situation.

    ViewFormulaGood for
    1. Dot products$(AB)_{ij} = (\text{row } i \text{ of } A)\cdot(\text{col } j \text{ of } B)$computing one entry; attention scores $QK^\top$
    2. Column combinationscol $j$ of $AB$ $= A\,(\text{col } j \text{ of } B) = \sum_k B_{kj}\,\mathbf{a}_k$the transformation view (section 1); column space
    3. Row combinationsrow $i$ of $AB$ $= \sum_k A_{ik}\,(\text{row } k \text{ of } B)$row operations in elimination (section 5)
    4. Sum of outer products$AB = \sum_k \mathbf{a}_k\,\mathbf{b}_k^{\top}$ (col $k$ of $A$ times row $k$ of $B$)low-rank structure, SVD (lesson 04), gradients (lesson 07)

    View 4 is the least familiar. An outer product $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top$ of a column and a row is a full matrix whose $(i,j)$ entry is $u_iv_j$. Every row is a multiple of $\mathbf{v}^\top$, so it has rank 1 (section 7). View 4 says every product $AB$ is a sum of $n$ such rank-1 layers.

    Try it: pick a view and press step to build $C = AB$ piece by piece. Highlighted cells are the ones in use. In the dot-product view each step fills one entry; in the column and row views each step fills a whole column or row; in the outer-product view each step adds a full rank-1 layer, and $C$ is only correct after the last one. All four end at the same $C$.
    Worked example: one product, four ways

    $A = \begin{bmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&-1\end{bmatrix}$ ($2\times3$), $B = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\2&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$ ($3\times2$). The result is $2\times2$.

    Dot products. $C_{11} = (1,2,0)\cdot(1,2,0) = 5$, $C_{12} = (1,2,0)\cdot(1,0,3) = 1$, $C_{21} = (0,1,-1)\cdot(1,2,0) = 2$, $C_{22} = (0,1,-1)\cdot(1,0,3) = -3$.

    Columns. Column 1 of $C$ $= 1\cdot\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} + 2\cdot\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix} + 0\cdot\begin{bmatrix}0\\-1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}5\\2\end{bmatrix}$.

    Rows. Row 2 of $C$ $= 0\cdot(1,1) + 1\cdot(2,0) + (-1)\cdot(0,3) = (2,-3)$.

    Outer products. $\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}(1,1) + \begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}(2,0) + \begin{bmatrix}0\\-1\end{bmatrix}(0,3) = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}4&0\\2&0\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&-3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}5&1\\2&-3\end{bmatrix}.$

    4. Linear systems: the row picture and the column picture

    A system of linear equations is a single matrix equation. For example

    $$\begin{aligned} 2x + y &= 5 \\ x - y &= 1 \end{aligned} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}}_{A}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}}.$$

    There are two ways to picture it, matching views 1 and 2 above.

    • Row picture. Each row is one equation, and each equation is a line in the $(x,y)$ plane (in 3-D, a plane). The solution is where all the lines meet. Here they meet at $(2, 1)$.
    • Column picture. $A\mathbf{x} = x\,\mathbf{a}_1 + y\,\mathbf{a}_2$. The question becomes: which combination of the columns produces $\mathbf{b}$? Here $2\cdot(2,1) + 1\cdot(1,-1) = (5,1)$. ✓

    The column picture is the one that scales to understanding. $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has a solution exactly when $\mathbf{b}$ is in the span of the columns (lesson 01). In the language of lesson 02: when $\mathbf{b}$ is somewhere the transformation $A$ can reach.

    Try it: right panel (column picture): drag the columns a₁, a₂ and the target b. The faint arrows show $x\,\mathbf{a}_1$ followed by $y\,\mathbf{a}_2$ landing exactly on $\mathbf{b}$. The left panel (row picture) shows the same system as two lines meeting at $(x, y)$. Now drag $\mathbf{a}_2$ onto the line through $\mathbf{a}_1$. In the row picture the lines go parallel. In the column picture the columns only reach a line, and unless $\mathbf{b}$ is on it there's no solution.

    For a square $A$ there are only two possibilities. If $\det A \ne 0$, the columns span everything and there is exactly one solution for every $\mathbf{b}$. If $\det A = 0$, there are none or infinitely many, depending on $\mathbf{b}$. "Exactly two solutions" never happens: if $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ both solve it, so does every point on the line through them.

    5. Gaussian elimination

    Drawing pictures doesn't scale past 3 unknowns. The algorithm that does is Gaussian elimination, and it's the one you learned in school with a bit more discipline. Three row operations never change the solution set:

    1. Swap two equations.
    2. Multiply an equation by a non-zero number.
    3. Subtract a multiple of one equation from another.

    Each is "multiply both sides on the left by an invertible matrix" (view 3: left-multiplication combines rows). So they can't create or destroy solutions.

    The plan: write the augmented matrix $[A\,|\,\mathbf{b}]$. For each column $k$, pick a pivot in row $k$, and subtract multiples of the pivot row to zero out everything below it. The multiplier for row $i$ is $\ell_{ik} = a_{ik}/a_{kk}$. You end with an upper-triangular system $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{c}$, which back-substitution solves from the bottom up.

    Worked example: elimination by hand

    Solve $x+2y+z = 4$, $2x+5y+3z = 11$, $x+3y+4z = 9$.

    $$\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&2&1&4\\2&5&3&11\\1&3&4&9\end{array}\right] \xrightarrow[R_3 - R_1]{R_2 - 2R_1} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&2&1&4\\0&1&1&3\\0&1&3&5\end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_3 - R_2} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}1&2&1&4\\0&1&1&3\\0&0&2&2\end{array}\right]$$

    Back-substitute: $2z = 2 \Rightarrow z = 1$. Then $y + 1 = 3 \Rightarrow y = 2$. Then $x + 4 + 1 = 4 \Rightarrow x = -1$.

    Check in the original third equation: $-1 + 6 + 4 = 9$. ✓ The pivots were $1, 1, 2$, and their product is $\det A = 2$. That's no accident: row operation 3 doesn't change the determinant, and the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal.

    Partial pivoting

    If a pivot is zero, you can't divide by it, so you swap in a row from below. In floating point you should swap even when the pivot is merely small: dividing by $10^{-20}$ makes huge multipliers that swamp the other numbers in rounding error. Partial pivoting always picks the largest $|a_{ik}|$ in the column as the pivot. Every serious solver does this. If no row has a usable pivot, the column is a combination of earlier ones and the matrix is singular.

    Try it: press step to run elimination on $[A\,|\,\mathbf{b}]$. The boxed cell is the current pivot and the tinted row is the one being changed. Then watch back-substitution read off the solution. Switch to "zero pivot" and untick partial pivoting: the naive algorithm hits a 0 on the diagonal and stops. Tick it again and it swaps rows and carries on. "Singular" fails either way, because no pivot exists.

    Elimination costs about $\tfrac23 n^3$ operations: for each of $n$ pivots, update an $(n-k)\times(n-k)$ block. Recording the multipliers $\ell_{ik}$ in a lower-triangular $L$ gives the factorization $PA = LU$ ($P$ records the swaps). Factor once, then each new right-hand side costs only $O(n^2)$. That's what np.linalg.solve does under the hood (LAPACK's gesv).

    6. The inverse: undo

    If $A$ is a transformation, its inverse $A^{-1}$ is the transformation that puts everything back:

    $$A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I.$$

    Then $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has solution $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$. For a $2\times2$ matrix there's a closed form. Multiply out to check it:

    $$\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}ad-bc&0\\0&ad-bc\end{bmatrix}.$$

    The determinant appears in the denominator, so the inverse exists exactly when $\det A \ne 0$. Geometrically: if $A$ squashes the plane onto a line (area scale 0), many points land on the same output, and no function can tell them apart to send them back.

    For bigger matrices, use Gauss–Jordan. Row-reduce $[A\,|\,I]$ all the way until the left half is $I$, eliminating above each pivot as well as below. The row operations multiply to $A^{-1}$, so the right half becomes $A^{-1}I = A^{-1}$.

    Two rules worth deriving rather than memorizing:

    • $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$. To undo "put on socks, then shoes", take off the shoes first. Check: $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I$.
    • $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A$. The undo must un-scale area.
    Try it: left is the input space, right is the output after $A$. Drag the column handles Aî and Aĵ on the right, and the probe point x on the left. The dashed outline on the left is $A^{-1}(A\cdot\text{shape})$, and it sits exactly on the original: the undo works. Now press "make singular", or line the two columns up yourself. The output collapses onto a line (the column space). On the left, a whole line of inputs (the dashed one through $\mathbf{x}$, parallel to the null space) now maps to the single output $A\mathbf{x}$, and the undo is gone.
    Worked example: a 2×2 inverse

    $A = \begin{bmatrix}4&7\\2&6\end{bmatrix}$. $\det A = 24 - 14 = 10$, so

    $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix}6&-7\\-2&4\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0.6&-0.7\\-0.2&0.4\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Check the first column of $AA^{-1}$: $A(0.6,-0.2) = (2.4-1.4,\;1.2-1.2) = (1, 0)$. ✓

    In numerical code, don't compute $A^{-1}$ just to solve $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$. Use np.linalg.solve(A, b). Forming the inverse costs about 3× more and is less accurate. The inverse is a thinking tool far more often than a computing tool.

    7. Rank, column space and null space

    "Singular or not" is too coarse for non-square matrices, or for asking how singular. Three linked ideas fill the gap. Let $A$ be $m\times n$.

    • The column space $C(A)$ is the span of the columns, everything $A\mathbf{x}$ can reach. It lives in $\mathbb{R}^m$. $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ is solvable iff $\mathbf{b}\in C(A)$.
    • The rank is $\dim C(A)$: the number of independent columns. It's the dimension of the output, after any squashing.
    • The null space $N(A) = \{\mathbf{x} : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\}$ is everything $A$ crushes to zero. It lives in $\mathbb{R}^n$. If $\mathbf{x}_0$ solves $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$, then so does $\mathbf{x}_0 + \mathbf{n}$ for every $\mathbf{n}\in N(A)$. The null space is exactly the ambiguity in the answer.

    Elimination finds all three. Keep row-reducing until each pivot is 1 and is the only non-zero in its column. That's the reduced row echelon form (RREF). Columns with a pivot are pivot columns; the rest are free columns.

    • rank = number of pivots.
    • The pivot columns of the original $A$ are a basis for $C(A)$.
    • Each free column gives one null-space basis vector: set that free variable to 1, the other free variables to 0, and solve for the pivot variables.

    Each of the $n$ columns is either a pivot or free, which gives the rank–nullity theorem:

    $$\operatorname{rank}(A) + \dim N(A) = n.$$

    In words: the input dimensions that survive, plus the ones crushed to zero, add up to all of them.

    Worked example: RREF, rank and null space

    $A = \begin{bmatrix}1&2&1&1\\2&4&0&6\\1&2&2&-1\end{bmatrix}$ ($3\times4$).

    $R_2 - 2R_1$ and $R_3 - R_1$ give rows $(0,0,-2,4)$ and $(0,0,1,-2)$. Column 2 has no pivot below row 1, so it's free. Scale row 2 by $-\tfrac12$ to get $(0,0,1,-2)$, subtract it from row 3 (leaving zeros) and from row 1:

    $$R = \begin{bmatrix}1&2&0&3\\0&0&1&-2\\0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.$$

    Pivots are in columns 1 and 3, so rank 2. The column space is spanned by $(1,2,1)$ and $(1,0,2)$, a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Free columns are 2 and 4, so the null space has dimension $4 - 2 = 2$.

    The rows of $R$ say $x_1 = -2x_2 - 3x_4$ and $x_3 = 2x_4$. Setting $(x_2,x_4) = (1,0)$ gives $(-2,1,0,0)$; setting $(0,1)$ gives $(-3,0,2,1)$. Check the second: $A(-3,0,2,1) = (-3+0+2+1,\; -6+0+0+6,\; -3+0+4-1) = (0,0,0)$. ✓

    Rank is a capacity limit. A product $UV$ with $U$ of shape $d\times r$ and $V$ of shape $r\times d$ has rank at most $r$, however large $d$ is. Each attention head projects to a small dimension, so its $QK^\top$ score matrix is low-rank. LoRA fine-tunes a big model by learning only a low-rank update $\Delta W = UV$ (lesson 25). Lesson 04 shows how the SVD finds the best low-rank approximation of any matrix.

    8. When there's no solution: a least-squares teaser

    Real data gives tall systems: more equations (data points) than unknowns (parameters). Then $\mathbf{b}$ is almost never in the column space, and $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no exact solution. The useful question becomes: which $\mathbf{x}$ makes $A\mathbf{x}$ as close to $\mathbf{b}$ as possible?

    The closest point in the column space is the projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto it, exactly as in lesson 01. The residual $\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is perpendicular to every column, so $A^\top(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0}$, which gives the normal equations:

    $$A^\top A\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top\mathbf{b}.$$

    That's a square system you now know how to solve. With one column $\mathbf{a}$ it reduces to $\hat x = \dfrac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}}$, the projection coefficient from lesson 01.

    Try it: here $A$ is a single column $\mathbf{a}$ (a $2\times1$ matrix), so its column space is the dashed line. Drag $\mathbf{b}$ off the line: no $x$ solves $x\,\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{b}$. Slide $x$ and watch the error $\|x\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\|$. It is smallest exactly at $\hat x$, where the error vector meets the line at a right angle. Press "jump to x̂" to snap there.

    Lesson 13 builds linear regression on exactly this, and shows why $A^\top A$ is invertible precisely when the columns of $A$ are independent (problem 8).

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    Let $A$ be $3\times4$, $B$ be $4\times2$ and $C$ be $2\times3$. Which of $AB$, $BA$, $BC$, $CA$, $ABC$, $CAB$ are defined, and what shape is each?

    Solution

    $AB$: $3\times2$. $BA$: $(4\times2)(3\times4)$, inner 2 ≠ 3, undefined. $BC$: $4\times3$. $CA$: $(2\times3)(3\times4) = 2\times4$. $ABC$: $3\times3$. $CAB$: $(2\times3)(3\times4)(4\times2) = 2\times2$. $ABC$ and $CAB$ are both square but different sizes. Order matters for shape, not only for values.

    Problem 3

    $(AB)C$: first $3\cdot4\cdot2$ to form $AB$ ($3\times2$), then multiply that by $C$.

    Solution

    $(AB)C$: $3\cdot4\cdot2 = 24$ for $AB$, then $(3\times2)(2\times3)$ costs $18$. Total $42$. $A(BC)$: $4\cdot2\cdot3 = 24$ for $BC$ ($4\times3$), then $(3\times4)(4\times3)$ costs $36$.     core

    Problem 6
    core · proof

    Prove (a) $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$ using the index formula, and (b) if $A$ and $B$ are invertible then so is $AB$, with $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$. (c) Why can't it be $A^{-1}B^{-1}$?

    Solution

    (a) $\big((AB)^\top\big)_{ij} = (AB)_{ji} = \sum_k A_{jk}B_{ki} = \sum_k (B^\top)_{ik}(A^\top)_{kj} = (B^\top A^\top)_{ij}$.

    (b) $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = I$ by associativity, and similarly on the other side.

    (c) $(AB)(A^{-1}B^{-1})$ doesn't simplify, because $B$ and $A^{-1}$ aren't adjacent. Geometrically, the last thing done must be the first thing undone.

    Problem 7
    core

    Let $M = \begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\\1&1&1\end{bmatrix}$. Find its rank and a basis for its null space. Enter the rank, then the null-space vector scaled so its first entry is 1.

    Hint

    Row 2 is twice row 1. Eliminate, then find the free column.

    Solution

    $R_2 - 2R_1 = \mathbf{0}$ and $R_3 - R_1 = (0,-1,-2)$. Swap and clean up to get $\text{RREF} = \begin{bmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&1&2\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}$. Two pivots, so rank 2, and column 3 is free. Set $x_3 = 1$: $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = -2$. Null space is spanned by $(1,-2,1)$. Check: $1-4+3 = 0$ and $1-2+1 = 0$. ✓ Rank–nullity: $2 + 1 = 3$.

    Problem 8
    stretch · proof

    Let $A$ be $m\times n$. Prove that $N(A^\top A) = N(A)$, and conclude that $A^\top A$ is invertible exactly when the columns of $A$ are independent. (This is what makes the normal equations of section 8 solvable.)

    Hint

    One direction is immediate. For the other, if $A^\top A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, take the dot product with $\mathbf{x}$ and recognize a squared norm.

    Solution

    If $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ then $A^\top A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Conversely, if $A^\top A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ then $0 = \mathbf{x}^\top A^\top A\mathbf{x} = (A\mathbf{x})^\top(A\mathbf{x}) = \|A\mathbf{x}\|^2$, so $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. The null spaces are equal. $A^\top A$ is square ($n\times n$), so it's invertible iff its null space is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, iff $N(A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$, iff the columns of $A$ are independent (no non-trivial combination gives zero).

    Problem 9
    stretch

    A layer maps $\mathbb{R}^{512}\to\mathbb{R}^{512}$ with $W = UV$, where $U$ is $512\times16$ and $V$ is $16\times512$. (a) What is the largest possible rank of $W$? (b) What is the smallest possible dimension of $N(W)$? (c) How many parameters do $U, V$ have compared to a full $W$? Enter (a), (b), and the ratio (full)/(factored).

    Solution

    (a) Every column of $W = UV$ is $U$ times something, so $C(W)\subseteq C(U)$, which has dimension at most 16. (b) Rank–nullity: $\dim N(W) = 512 - \operatorname{rank} \ge 496$. At least 496 input directions are thrown away completely. (c) Full: $512^2 = 262{,}144$. Factored: $2\cdot512\cdot16 = 16{,}384$. Ratio 16. This is the trade LoRA makes (lesson 25).

    10. Quick check

    You know $AB = AC$. When can you conclude $B = C$?
    $A(B - C) = 0$ says every column of $B - C$ is in $N(A)$. If the null space is just $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, then $B = C$. Otherwise $B$ and $C$ can differ by null-space vectors. Example: $A = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ can't tell apart matrices that differ only in their second row.
    $A$ is $3\times3$ with $\det A = 0$. What can you say about $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$?
    Singular means a non-trivial null space and a column space smaller than $\mathbb{R}^3$. If $\mathbf{b}$ is outside $C(A)$: no solution. If it's inside, any one solution plus any null-space vector is another, so infinitely many.
    A $5\times3$ matrix has rank 3. Which statement is true?
    Rank–nullity uses $n = 3$ (columns): nullity $= 3 - 3 = 0$. Three columns can span at most 3 dimensions of $\mathbb{R}^5$, so most $\mathbf{b}$ are unreachable (that's the least-squares setting). Non-square matrices don't have two-sided inverses.
    Why does every serious solver use partial pivoting?
    In exact arithmetic, any non-zero pivot works. In floating point, $|\ell_{ik}| = |a_{ik}/a_{kk}| \le 1$ when $a_{kk}$ is the largest in its column, which keeps errors from blowing up. The cost is the same $O(n^3)$.
