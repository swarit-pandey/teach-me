Part 3 · Probability & statistics

Probability & random variables

A language model never says "the next word is mat". It says "mat with probability 0.48, floor with 0.21, …" and then rolls a die. To understand what a model outputs, how it is trained, and how it generates text, you need the language of probability. This lesson builds it from one picture: probability is area.

By the end you can

  • Describe an experiment by its sample space and events, and compute probabilities by counting area.
  • Explain conditional probability as "shrinking the world to B and renormalizing", and test events for independence.
  • Apply Bayes' rule with natural frequencies, and explain why a positive test for a rare disease usually means you are healthy.
  • Work with PMFs, CDFs and densities, including the Bernoulli, binomial and categorical distributions.
  • Sample from any discrete distribution with one uniform random number (exactly what an LLM does for every token).
  • State the law of large numbers and use it for Monte Carlo estimation.

    1. Sample spaces, events and probability as area

    Every probability question starts with an experiment: roll two dice, flip a coin ten times, let a model pick the next token. The set of all possible outcomes is the sample space $\Omega$ (omega). For two dice,

    $$\Omega = \{(1,1), (1,2), \dots, (6,6)\}, \qquad |\Omega| = 36.$$

    An event is any subset of $\Omega$: a yes/no question about the outcome. "The sum is 7" is the event $\{(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)\}$. A probability $P$ assigns each event a number, following three rules (Kolmogorov's axioms):

    1. $P(A) \ge 0$ for every event $A$.
    2. $P(\Omega) = 1$: something happens.
    3. If $A$ and $B$ are disjoint (can't both happen), $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$.

    That's all. Think of $\Omega$ as a square of total area 1, and $P(A)$ as the area of region $A$. The rules say area is non-negative, the whole square has area 1, and areas of non-overlapping regions add. When all outcomes are equally likely, as with fair dice, area is just counting:

    $$P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}.$$

    Two consequences fall straight out of the axioms. The complement "not $A$", written $A^c$, is disjoint from $A$ and together they make $\Omega$, so $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$. And for overlapping events, adding the two areas counts the overlap twice, so you subtract it once (inclusion–exclusion):

    $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).$$
    Try it: each cell is one of the 36 outcomes (the number in it is the sum). Pick events A and B. Their overlap $A\cap B$ is shaded purple. Choose "custom" and click cells to build your own event. Then tick condition on B: everything outside B greys out, and $P(A\mid B)$ is the share of B that A covers (section 2). Finally, find a pair where the bars on the right have equal length. Those events are independent (section 3).

    2. Conditional probability: shrinking the world

    You roll two dice but only see the first one: it's a 6. What's the probability the sum is at least 9? Your knowledge changed, so the probability should change too.

    Here's the reasoning, step by step. Learning "B happened" (first die is 6) does two things:

    1. Outcomes outside $B$ become impossible. Delete them.
    2. Outcomes inside $B$ keep their relative likelihoods. Nothing you learned favours $(6,3)$ over $(6,1)$.

    So the new probability of an outcome inside $B$ is its old probability scaled up by a constant, chosen so that the total is 1 again. The surviving area is $P(B)$, so the constant is $1/P(B)$. The part of $A$ that survives is $A\cap B$. Therefore:

    $$\boxed{\;P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\;}\qquad (P(B) > 0)$$

    Read "$P(A\mid B)$" as "probability of $A$ given $B$". Geometrically it is the fraction of B's area that A covers. In the dice figure, condition on "first die is 6": B is the rightmost column of 6 cells, and 4 of them have sum $\ge 9$, so $P(A\mid B) = 4/6 \approx 0.667$, up from $P(A) = 10/36 \approx 0.278$.

    Rearranging gives the product rule, which is how you compute joint probabilities in practice:

    $$P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A).$$

    Apply it repeatedly and you get the chain rule of probability:

    $$P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = P(x_1)\,P(x_2 \mid x_1)\,P(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2)\cdots P(x_n \mid x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}).$$

    The chain rule is the entire design of GPT-style language models. The probability of a sentence is a product of next-token probabilities, each conditioned on everything before it. A model that can output $P(\text{next token} \mid \text{previous tokens})$ therefore defines a probability for every possible text. You'll build one in lesson 24, and training it means making these conditional probabilities large on real text (lessons 10 and 11).

    One more tool. If events $B_1, \dots, B_k$ split $\Omega$ into non-overlapping pieces, then $A$ is split into the pieces $A\cap B_i$, and the areas add. With the product rule, that gives the law of total probability:

    $$P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i).$$

    "Average the conditional probabilities, weighted by how likely each case is." You'll use it in Bayes' rule in a moment.

    Worked example

    Two dice. Let $A$ = "sum is 7", $B$ = "first die is 3". Then $A\cap B = \{(3,4)\}$, a single cell, so $P(A\cap B) = 1/36$. $B$ is one column: $P(B) = 6/36$. So

    $$P(A\mid B) = \frac{1/36}{6/36} = \frac16.$$

    Unconditionally, $P(A) = 6/36 = 1/6$ too. Knowing the first die is 3 told you nothing about whether the sum is 7, because whatever the first die shows, exactly one value of the second die makes 7. Hold that thought.

    3. Independence

    Events $A$ and $B$ are independent when learning one doesn't change the probability of the other: $P(A\mid B) = P(A)$. Substitute the definition of conditional probability and multiply through by $P(B)$ to get the symmetric form most people use:

    $$A, B \text{ independent} \iff P(A\cap B) = P(A)\,P(B).$$

    In the area picture: A occupies the same fraction of B as it does of the whole square. In the dice figure, "first die even" and "sum is 7" are independent: $P(A\cap B) = 3/36 = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac16$. "Sum $\ge 9$" and "first die is 6" are not.

    Disjoint is not independent. In fact it's the opposite. If $A$ and $B$ can't both happen and each has positive probability, then learning $B$ happened tells you $A$ definitely didn't: $P(A\mid B) = 0 \ne P(A)$. Disjoint events with positive probability are always dependent.

    Independence is what lets you multiply. Flip a fair coin 10 times and the probability of one specific sequence, like HHTHTTTHHT, is $(1/2)^{10}$, because the flips are independent. Machine learning leans on this hard. Training data is assumed to be i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed), so the probability of the whole dataset is a product of per-example probabilities. Take the log, and that product becomes the sum you minimize as a loss (lesson 10).

    4. Bayes' rule with 1000 people

    The product rule says $P(A\mid B)P(B) = P(B\mid A)P(A)$. Divide by $P(B)$ and you have Bayes' rule, the formula for flipping a conditional around:

    $$\boxed{\;P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}\;} \qquad P(E) = P(E\mid H)P(H) + P(E\mid H^c)P(H^c).$$

    Read $H$ as a hypothesis and $E$ as evidence. The names matter, because you'll use them for the rest of the course:

    TermSymbolMeaning
    Prior$P(H)$how plausible $H$ was before the evidence
    Likelihood$P(E\mid H)$how well $H$ predicts the evidence
    Evidence$P(E)$total probability of the evidence, under every hypothesis (law of total probability)
    Posterior$P(H\mid E)$how plausible $H$ is after the evidence

    The classic trap: a disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 90% of sick people (sensitivity $P(+\mid D) = 0.9$) and correctly clears 91% of healthy people (specificity $P(-\mid \text{healthy}) = 0.91$). You test positive. How worried should you be? Most people, including many doctors in published studies, answer "about 90%". The formula is easy to misapply, so don't start with it. Count people instead.

    Try it: each square is one of 1000 people. Tick show only positive tests: that's conditioning on the evidence. The bar at the bottom shows who is left: the posterior is the pink fraction. Now drag the prevalence up to 20% and watch the posterior climb, even though the test hasn't changed. Then put prevalence back to 1% and raise the specificity to 99.5%. Which slider matters most for a rare disease?
    Worked example: the medical test

    Of 1000 people, $1000 \times 0.01 = 10$ are sick and 990 are healthy.

    Sick and positive (true positives): $10 \times 0.9 = 9$. Healthy but positive (false positives): $990 \times (1-0.91) = 89.1$.

    Among everyone who tests positive, the sick fraction is

    $$P(D\mid +) = \frac{9}{9 + 89.1} \approx 0.092.$$

    About 9%, not 90%. The test is good, but the healthy group is 99 times larger, so its 9% false-positive rate still produces ten times more positives than the sick group does. The same calculation in Bayes form is $\frac{0.9 \times 0.01}{0.9\times0.01 + 0.09\times0.99}$. It's identical, just with every count divided by 1000.

    The base rate is evidence too. A posterior blends what you knew (the prior) with what you saw (the likelihood). When the prior is extreme, it takes strong evidence to overcome it. A second, independent positive test takes the posterior to about 50% (problem 8). Each test's posterior becomes the next test's prior. That is Bayesian updating, and you'll do it continuously for a coin's bias in lesson 10.

    5. Random variables, PMFs and CDFs

    Events are yes/no questions. Usually you want a number: the sum of the dice, the number of heads, the index of the sampled token. A random variable $X$ is a function from outcomes to numbers, $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$. "Sum of two dice" maps $(3,4) \mapsto 7$. Despite the name, nothing about $X$ is random. The randomness lives in which outcome occurs; $X$ just reads off a number.

    A discrete random variable takes countably many values. Its probability mass function (PMF) gives the probability of each value:

    $$p_X(x) = P(X = x), \qquad p_X(x) \ge 0, \qquad \sum_x p_X(x) = 1.$$

    Its cumulative distribution function (CDF) accumulates the PMF from the left:

    $$F_X(x) = P(X \le x) = \sum_{x' \le x} p_X(x').$$

    The CDF is a staircase that climbs from 0 to 1. It jumps at each possible value, by exactly that value's probability. It never decreases.

    Three distributions to know

    Bernoulli($p$): one yes/no trial. $X = 1$ with probability $p$, else $0$. A coin flip, a click/no-click, "is this email spam".

    Binomial($n, p$): the number of successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli($p$) trials. The PMF comes from two observations:

    • Any specific sequence with $k$ successes and $n-k$ failures (like SSFSF…) has probability $p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$, because the trials are independent and you multiply.
    • There are $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$ such sequences: choose which $k$ of the $n$ positions are successes. They are disjoint, so their probabilities add.
    $$P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k}\, p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n.$$

    Categorical($\pi_1,\dots,\pi_K$): one draw from $K$ labelled outcomes with probabilities $\pi_i$ that sum to 1. Bernoulli is the $K=2$ case. This is the distribution an LLM outputs, so it gets its own section next.

    Try it: left is the PMF, right is the CDF. Drag the orange handle along the CDF's x-axis. The PMF bars at or left of it turn orange, and their total is exactly the height of the CDF there. For the binomial, slide $p$ and $n$: the hump sits near $np$ and gets wider as $n$ grows. Note where the CDF jumps (at each value) and where it is flat (between values).
    Worked example

    Flip a fair coin 4 times. What's the probability of exactly 2 heads? $X\sim\text{Binomial}(4, \tfrac12)$:

    $$P(X=2) = \binom42 \left(\tfrac12\right)^2\left(\tfrac12\right)^2 = 6 \cdot \tfrac1{16} = 0.375.$$

    Check by listing: HHTT, HTHT, HTTH, THHT, THTH, TTHH. That's 6 of the 16 equally likely sequences. And $P(X \le 1) = F(1) = \binom40/16 + \binom41/16 = 5/16$.

    6. The categorical distribution: how an LLM picks a token

    At every step, a language model's final layer outputs one number per vocabulary entry (50,257 of them for GPT-2), called logits. A softmax (lesson 14) turns logits into a categorical distribution $\pi$ over the vocabulary. Then the model samples from it. How do you sample from an arbitrary categorical using only a random number generator that gives uniform numbers in $[0, 1)$?

    Lay the probabilities end to end along $[0,1]$: token 1 gets $[0, \pi_1)$, token 2 gets $[\pi_1, \pi_1+\pi_2)$, and so on. The breakpoints are the CDF values $F_1, F_2, \dots, F_K = 1$. Draw $u \sim \text{Uniform}[0,1)$ and return the token whose segment $u$ lands in:

    $$\text{return the smallest } i \text{ with } u \lt F_i.$$

    Why it's correct: a uniform $u$ lands in an interval with probability equal to the interval's length, and token $i$'s interval has length $F_i - F_{i-1} = \pi_i$. This is inverse-CDF sampling. It works for any distribution whose CDF you can invert, including continuous ones (section 7).

    Try it: press sample 1 a few times. Watch $u$ land on the strip and pick the token whose segment it hits. Then press sample 100 repeatedly: the solid bars (fraction of your samples) converge to the outlined bars (the model's probabilities). Now move the temperature, which divides the logits before the softmax. Low temperature makes the model nearly greedy; high temperature flattens the distribution toward uniform. Lesson 25 covers temperature, top-k and nucleus sampling.

    "Generating text" is just this loop: compute logits, softmax into a categorical, draw $u$, look up the token, append it, repeat. All of a model's creativity and variety comes from that one uniform random number per token. Set temperature to 0 (always take the most likely token, called greedy decoding) and the same prompt always gives the same output.

    7. Continuous random variables and densities

    Some quantities vary continuously: a height, a model weight, the time until a server request arrives. For these, any exact value has probability zero. The chance a height is exactly 1.7500000… m is nil. So instead of a mass at each point, a continuous random variable has a probability density function (PDF) $f(x)$, and probabilities are areas under it:

    $$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \qquad f(x)\ge 0, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1.$$

    The CDF is the same idea as before, with the sum replaced by an integral: $F(x) = P(X\le x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$. By the fundamental theorem of calculus (lesson 05), $f = F'$. Density is the rate at which probability accumulates.

    A density is not a probability and can be larger than 1. Uniform on $[0, 0.5]$ has $f(x) = 2$ on that interval: the area is $0.5 \times 2 = 1$, as required. The units of $f$ are "probability per unit of $x$". Only $f(x)\,dx$, the area of a thin sliver, is a probability.

    Two densities to know now (the Gaussian gets a full treatment in lesson 09):

    • Uniform$(a,b)$: $f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}$ on $[a,b]$. Every equal-width sub-interval is equally likely.
    • Exponential$(\lambda)$: $f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x\ge0$, the waiting time between random events. Its CDF is $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$, and inverting it gives a sampler: $X = -\ln(1-u)/\lambda$ with $u$ uniform. That is inverse-CDF sampling again, now in its continuous form.
    Try it: drag the two orange handles. The shaded area is $P(a\le X\le b)$, and the readout compares it to the fraction of simulated samples that fell in $[a,b]$. Raise the number of samples and the number of bins together: the normalized histogram turns into the density curve. That's what a density is, the limit of a histogram. Switch to Uniform[0, 0.5] to see a density of 2.
    Worked example

    Server requests arrive with exponential waiting times, $\lambda = 1$ per second. What's the probability the next one arrives between 1 and 2 seconds from now?

    $$P(1\le X\le 2) = F(2) - F(1) = (1-e^{-2}) - (1-e^{-1}) = e^{-1} - e^{-2} \approx 0.3679 - 0.1353 = 0.2325.$$

    Set the handles to 1 and 2 in the figure (with the exponential selected) to check.

    8. The law of large numbers and Monte Carlo

    What does "$P(\text{heads}) = 0.3$" even mean? The frequentist answer: flip many times, and the fraction of heads settles down to 0.3. The law of large numbers (LLN) makes this precise. If $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ (the long-run average value, defined properly as the expectation in lesson 09), then the running average

    $$\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \;\longrightarrow\; \mu \quad\text{as } n\to\infty.$$

    Fluctuations don't disappear; they get diluted. A run of five sixes moves the average a lot after 10 rolls and barely at all after 10,000. The typical distance from $\mu$ shrinks like $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, where $\sigma$ measures the spread of a single roll. Lesson 09 derives this. The square root is a hard law of nature: 10× more accuracy costs 100× more samples.

    Try it: press play. Five independent experiments roll a fair die and track their running average (the x-axis is logarithmic: 1, 10, 100, 1000 rolls). Early on the paths swing wildly; later they all squeeze toward 3.5, staying mostly inside the dashed $\mu \pm \sigma/\sqrt{n}$ funnel. Switch to the biased coin: the running average of 0/1 outcomes is the fraction of heads, converging to $p = 0.3$.

    Turned around, the LLN is a computational tool: Monte Carlo estimation. To find the probability of a complicated event, simulate the experiment many times and count. To compute an average, take samples and average them. Here's a famous example. Throw $n$ random points into the unit square. The fraction landing inside the quarter circle $x^2+y^2\lt 1$ estimates its area, $\pi/4$. You'll do exactly this in the workbook, and compare simulated dice probabilities with exact counts.

    Every training step in deep learning is a Monte Carlo estimate. The true loss is an average over all possible data. You can't compute it, so you average over a random minibatch of 32 or 1024 examples. That average is noisy but unbiased, with noise shrinking like $1/\sqrt{\text{batch size}}$ (lesson 18). Policy gradients in RL (lesson 27) estimate expected reward from sampled rollouts in exactly the same way.

    9. Problems

    Pencil first. Every answer box accepts arithmetic, so you can type 5/36.

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    Two fair dice. What is the probability that the sum is 8?

    Solution

    The outcomes are $(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2)$: 5 of 36. $P = 5/36 \approx 0.1389$.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    Roll one die 4 times. What is the probability of at least one six?

    Hint

    "At least one" is messy (one six, two sixes, …). Its complement "no sixes" is easy, and the rolls are independent.

    Solution

    $P(\text{no six}) = (5/6)^4 \approx 0.4823$, so $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - (5/6)^4 \approx 0.5177$. This was the 17th-century gambling bet that started probability theory (the Chevalier de Méré's problem).

    Problem 3
    core

    A disease has prevalence 2%. The test has sensitivity 95% and specificity 90%. You test positive. What is $P(\text{disease}\mid +)$?

    Solution

    Out of 1000 people: 20 sick, and 19 of them test positive. 980 healthy, and 10% of them (98) test positive anyway. $P = 19/(19+98) = 19/117 \approx 0.1624$.

    Problem 4
    core · proof

    (a) Show that for two dice, $A$ = "first die is even" and $B$ = "sum is 7" are independent. (b) Prove that if $A$ and $B$ are independent, so are $A$ and $B^c$.

    Solution

    (a) $P(A) = 18/36 = 1/2$ and $P(B) = 6/36 = 1/6$. $A\cap B = \{(2,5),(4,3),(6,1)\}$, so $P(A\cap B) = 3/36 = 1/12 = P(A)P(B)$. ✓

    (b) $A$ splits into the disjoint pieces $A\cap B$ and $A\cap B^c$, so $P(A\cap B^c) = P(A) - P(A\cap B) = P(A) - P(A)P(B) = P(A)(1-P(B)) = P(A)P(B^c)$. $\blacksquare$ Knowing that B didn't happen tells you nothing either.

    Problem 5
    core

    A model answers each question correctly with probability 0.3, independently. Out of 10 questions, what is the probability it gets exactly 3 right?

    Solution

    Binomial(10, 0.3): $\binom{10}{3}(0.3)^3(0.7)^7 = 120 \times 0.027 \times 0.0823543 \approx 0.2668$.

    Problem 6
    core

    A model's next-token distribution over tokens 1–4 is $\pi = (0.5, 0.2, 0.2, 0.1)$. Using inverse-CDF sampling, which token does each of the uniforms $u = 0.12,\ 0.55,\ 0.71,\ 0.95$ produce? Enter four token numbers.

    Solution

    The CDF breakpoints are $F = (0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 1.0)$. Return the first $i$ with $u\lt F_i$: $0.12\lt0.5 \to 1$; $0.55 \lt 0.7 \to 2$; $0.71\lt0.9 \to 3$; $0.95\lt1 \to 4$.

    Problem 7
    core

    A random variable has density $f(x) = c\,x^2$ on $[0, 1]$ (and 0 elsewhere). Find $c$, and then $P(X \le 0.5)$. Enter both.

    Solution

    The total area must be 1: $\int_0^1 c x^2\,dx = c/3 = 1$, so $c = 3$. Then $F(x) = x^3$ on $[0,1]$, so $P(X\le 0.5) = 0.125$. Note $f(1) = 3 > 1$. Densities can exceed 1.

    Problem 8
    stretch

    Back to the worked example: prevalence 1%, sensitivity 90%, specificity 91%. You test positive, so your posterior is about 0.0917. You take a second test, whose result is independent of the first given your true disease status, and it's also positive. What is your posterior now?

    Hint

    Yesterday's posterior is today's prior. Apply Bayes again with prior $9/98.1$. Alternatively, apply Bayes once with likelihoods $0.9^2$ and $0.09^2$.

    Solution

    Prior $q = 9/98.1 \approx 0.09174$. Then $P(D\mid ++) = \frac{0.9q}{0.9q + 0.09(1-q)} \approx 0.5025$. In one step: $\frac{0.81\times0.01}{0.81\times0.01 + 0.0081\times0.99} = \frac{0.0081}{0.0081+0.008019} \approx 0.5025$. Two positives get you to a coin flip. The order of the evidence doesn't matter, only the product of the likelihoods.

    Problem 9
    stretch

    Monte Carlo: you sample $(x, y)$ uniformly in the unit square and record whether $x^2 + y^2 \lt 1$. (a) What fraction of samples should land inside, in the limit? (b) Roughly how many more samples do you need to make the estimate of $\pi$ 10× more accurate?

    Solution

    (a) The quarter disc has area $\pi/4 \approx 0.7854$, and for a uniform point in the unit square, probability equals area. So $4\times$(fraction inside) $\to \pi$. (b) The error shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$, so 10× accuracy needs 100× the samples. Monte Carlo is simple and works in any dimension, but it converges slowly.

    10. Quick check

    A spam filter flags 99% of spam. A defence lawyer argues: "so if an email is flagged, it's 99% likely to be spam." What's wrong?
    Bayes' rule: $P(S\mid F) = P(F\mid S)P(S)/P(F)$. Swapping a conditional is the most common probability error in the wild. It even has a name: the prosecutor's fallacy.
    A density function has $f(2) = 3.5$. What does this tell you?
    A density is probability per unit length. Only areas under it are probabilities, and the area of a thin sliver is height × width.
    $A$ and $B$ are disjoint events, each with probability 0.3. Are they independent?
    If you learn $B$ happened, you know for certain that $A$ didn't. That's maximal dependence, not independence.
    A model's next-token probabilities are (0.6, 0.3, 0.1). It samples (not greedy) 1000 times from this same context. About how often does it emit the third token?
    Sampling from a categorical returns each token with its probability, and by the law of large numbers the fraction settles near 0.1. Only greedy decoding always picks token 1.
