Part 1 · Linear algebra

Eigenvectors, SVD & PCA

A matrix can look like a jumble of numbers, but almost every matrix secretly does something simple: it stretches space along a few special directions. Find those directions and the matrix becomes easy to understand, compress and compute with. That's what eigenvectors, the SVD and PCA give you.

By the end you can

  • Say what an eigenvector is geometrically, and find eigenvalues of a 2×2 matrix from $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$.
  • Explain why power iteration converges, and how fast.
  • State the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices and prove that their eigenvectors are orthogonal.
  • Read any matrix as rotate → scale → rotate (the SVD), and build its best rank-$k$ approximation.
  • Derive PCA as "find the direction of maximum variance", and compute it two ways: from the covariance's eigenvectors and from the SVD of the data.

    1. Directions that only scale

    In lesson 02 you saw that a 2×2 matrix $A$ moves every vector: the columns of $A$ say where $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$ land, and everything else follows by linearity. Most vectors get knocked off their original line. They are rotated as well as stretched.

    But usually a few special directions are not rotated. A vector pointing that way comes out pointing the same way (or exactly backwards), just longer or shorter. Those are the eigenvectors of $A$, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue:

    $$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}, \qquad \mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}.$$

    Read it as "applying $A$ to $\mathbf{v}$ is the same as multiplying $\mathbf{v}$ by the number $\lambda$". Along an eigenvector, a whole matrix collapses into a single scalar. If $\lambda \lt 0$ the vector flips; if $\lambda = 0$ it's crushed to the origin (it's in the null space from lesson 03).

    Any multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector: $A(c\mathbf{v}) = cA\mathbf{v} = \lambda(c\mathbf{v})$. So what matters is the line (the span), not one particular arrow. We usually pick the unit-length one.

    Try it: drag the blue and teal handles: they are $A\hat\imath$ and $A\hat\jmath$, the columns of $A$. The thin arrows show where 48 evenly spaced unit vectors land. The ones that stayed on their own line turn green, and the green bands are the exact eigen-directions. Drag the orange unit vector $\mathbf{x}$ around the circle and watch $A\mathbf{x}$ (pink) line up with it only on the bands. Then try the presets: the rotation has no green directions at all, and the shear has only one. Tick "symmetric" and the two bands snap to 90°.

    Think of eigenvectors as the "grain" of a transformation. Along the grain, $A$ is just a scaling; everywhere else it's a mix of scalings along the grain lines. That's why they make hard problems easy: write a vector in the eigenvector basis and applying $A$ becomes "multiply each coordinate by its $\lambda$".

    2. Finding them: the characteristic polynomial

    Move everything to one side: $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ is the same as

    $$(A - \lambda I)\,\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}.$$

    So the matrix $A - \lambda I$ sends a non-zero vector to zero. It has a non-trivial null space, so it's singular (lesson 03), so it squashes area to zero, so its determinant vanishes (lesson 02):

    $$\boxed{\;\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\;}$$

    For a 2×2 matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{bmatrix}$ this is a quadratic in $\lambda$:

    $$\det\begin{bmatrix} a-\lambda & b \\ c & d-\lambda\end{bmatrix} = (a-\lambda)(d-\lambda) - bc = \lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad-bc) = 0.$$

    The coefficients are the trace $\operatorname{tr}A = a+d$ and the determinant $\det A$. Since a monic quadratic with roots $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ factors as $(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2$, matching coefficients gives two facts that hold in every dimension:

    $$\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \operatorname{tr}A, \qquad \lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det A.$$

    The second one is lovely: the determinant is the area scaling factor (lesson 02), and along the eigen-directions the area gets scaled by $\lambda_1$ one way and $\lambda_2$ the other.

    Once you have a $\lambda$, the eigenvector is any non-zero vector in the null space of $A-\lambda I$. In 2-D that's easy: the rows of $A-\lambda I$ are parallel, and $\mathbf{v}$ must be orthogonal to them. So if a row is $(p, q)$, take $\mathbf{v} = (-q, p)$.

    Worked example 1

    $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}$. Trace $4$, determinant $3$, so $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda-3)(\lambda-1) = 0$, giving $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 1$.

    For $\lambda_1 = 3$: $A - 3I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}$. Its first row is $(-1, 1)$, so $\mathbf{v}_1 = (-1, -1) \propto (1, 1)$. Check: $A(1,1) = (3, 3) = 3\,(1,1)$. ✓

    For $\lambda_2 = 1$: $A - I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$, row $(1,1)$, so $\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1, 1)$. Check: $A(-1,1) = (-1, 1)$. ✓

    Sanity: $3 + 1 = 4 = \operatorname{tr}A$ and $3 \cdot 1 = 3 = \det A$. Notice $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 0$. That's no accident, because $A$ is symmetric (section 4). This is the "symmetric" preset in the figure above.

    Never compute eigenvalues of a big matrix by finding roots of its characteristic polynomial: polynomial roots are numerically fragile and the degree is $n$. Real libraries use iterative methods. The simplest one is next.

    3. Power iteration: let the matrix find its own eigenvector

    Take any starting vector $\mathbf{x}_0$ and keep hitting it with $A$, re-normalizing so it doesn't blow up:

    $$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \frac{A\mathbf{x}_k}{\|A\mathbf{x}_k\|}.$$

    It turns toward the eigenvector with the largest $|\lambda|$. Here's why. Suppose $A$ has eigenvectors $\mathbf{q}_1, \dots, \mathbf{q}_n$ forming a basis, with $|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \ge \cdots$. Write the start in that basis, $\mathbf{x}_0 = c_1\mathbf{q}_1 + \cdots + c_n\mathbf{q}_n$. Applying $A$ just multiplies each coordinate by its eigenvalue, so after $k$ steps (ignoring normalization, which only rescales):

    $$A^k\mathbf{x}_0 = c_1\lambda_1^k\mathbf{q}_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k\mathbf{q}_2 + \cdots = \lambda_1^k\Big( c_1\mathbf{q}_1 + c_2\big(\tfrac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\big)^k\mathbf{q}_2 + \cdots\Big).$$

    Every ratio $|\lambda_i/\lambda_1|$ is less than 1, so those terms die off geometrically and only the $\mathbf{q}_1$ direction survives (as long as $c_1 \neq 0$). The error shrinks by a factor of about $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|$ per step. Once $\mathbf{x}$ has converged, read off the eigenvalue with the Rayleigh quotient $\lambda_1 \approx \mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}$ (for unit $\mathbf{x}$).

    Try it: press Play. The orange iterate swings onto the dominant eigen-line (green, $\lambda_1 = 1$). The faint line is the second eigenvector, with eigenvalue $r$. On the right, the error (log scale) falls on a straight line with slope $\log_{10}|r|$, which is the dashed prediction. Drag $r$ toward ±1 and convergence slows to a crawl. With negative $r$ the iterate zig-zags from side to side as it closes in.

    Power iteration is everywhere. Google's original PageRank is power iteration on the web's link matrix. Spectral normalization in GANs runs one power-iteration step per training step to estimate a weight matrix's largest singular value. The "exploding/vanishing" behavior of deep and recurrent nets (lessons 18 and 20) is the same story: multiply by the same matrix many times and the largest eigenvalue takes over. If $|\lambda_1| > 1$ the signal explodes; if it's less than 1, it dies.

    4. Symmetric matrices: the spectral theorem

    Symmetric matrices ($A = A^\top$) are the best-behaved matrices there are, and they're everywhere in ML: covariance matrices, Hessians (lesson 07), Gram matrices $X^\top X$ (lesson 13), kernel matrices. For them, everything goes right.

    Spectral theorem. A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Stack them as the columns of an orthogonal matrix $Q$ ($Q^\top Q = I$) and put the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\Lambda$:

    $$A = Q\Lambda Q^\top = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top.$$

    The orthogonality is short enough to prove. Take two eigenpairs with $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$. Compute $\mathbf{q}_1^\top A\mathbf{q}_2$ two ways:

    $$\mathbf{q}_1^\top (A\mathbf{q}_2) = \lambda_2\,\mathbf{q}_1^\top\mathbf{q}_2, \qquad (A^\top\mathbf{q}_1)^\top\mathbf{q}_2 = (A\mathbf{q}_1)^\top\mathbf{q}_2 = \lambda_1\,\mathbf{q}_1^\top\mathbf{q}_2.$$

    The two left sides are equal because $A = A^\top$. Subtract: $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,\mathbf{q}_1^\top\mathbf{q}_2 = 0$. Since $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$, we get $\mathbf{q}_1 \cdot \mathbf{q}_2 = 0$. $\blacksquare$ (Real eigenvalues and the repeated-eigenvalue case need a bit more work; see Strang ch. 6.)

    Read $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ right-to-left as a recipe for applying $A$ to $\mathbf{x}$. $Q^\top\mathbf{x}$ finds the coordinates of $\mathbf{x}$ in the eigenbasis (each is a dot product $\mathbf{q}_i\cdot\mathbf{x}$, just like the projections of lesson 01). Then $\Lambda$ scales each coordinate by its $\lambda_i$. Then $Q$ goes back to the standard basis. So a symmetric matrix is rotate, scale along the axes, rotate back. It maps the unit circle to an ellipse whose axes are the eigenvectors, with semi-axis lengths $|\lambda_i|$.

    The outer-product form $\sum_i \lambda_i\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top$ is the "sum of outer products" view of matmul from lesson 03. Each term $\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top$ is the projection onto one eigen-line.

    5. The SVD: every matrix is rotate–scale–rotate

    Most matrices aren't symmetric, and many aren't even square. A $768\times3072$ weight matrix in a transformer has no eigenvectors at all, because $A\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ live in different spaces. But a slightly different decomposition always exists. The singular value decomposition (SVD) of any $m\times n$ matrix is

    $$A = U\Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top,$$

    where $V$ ($n\times n$) and $U$ ($m\times m$) are orthogonal, and $\Sigma$ is diagonal with singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0$. Geometrically: rotate (by $V^\top$), stretch along the coordinate axes (by $\Sigma$), rotate again (by $U$). The unit sphere always goes to an ellipsoid. Its semi-axes are $\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i$, and they are the images of the orthogonal input directions $\mathbf{v}_i$.

    Where it comes from

    Here's the trick: $A^\top A$ is square ($n\times n$) and symmetric, since $(A^\top A)^\top = A^\top A$. So by the spectral theorem it has orthonormal eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_i$ with real eigenvalues. Those eigenvalues are never negative:

    $$\lambda_i = \mathbf{v}_i^\top A^\top A\,\mathbf{v}_i = (A\mathbf{v}_i)^\top(A\mathbf{v}_i) = \|A\mathbf{v}_i\|^2 \ge 0.$$

    Define $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i} = \|A\mathbf{v}_i\|$, and for $\sigma_i > 0$ let $\mathbf{u}_i = A\mathbf{v}_i/\sigma_i$. The outputs are orthogonal too: $\mathbf{u}_i\cdot\mathbf{u}_j = \mathbf{v}_i^\top A^\top A\mathbf{v}_j/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = \lambda_j\,\mathbf{v}_i\cdot\mathbf{v}_j/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = 0$ for $i \ne j$. So $A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i$ for every $i$, which in matrix form is $AV = U\Sigma$, i.e. $A = U\Sigma V^\top$. $\blacksquare$

    In words: the SVD finds the orthogonal input directions that $A$ maps to orthogonal output directions.

    Try it: press Play (or scrub the stage slider) to watch $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ act in three moves. First $V^\top$ rotates the unit circle so the right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_1$ (blue) and $\mathbf{v}_2$ (orange) lie on the axes. Then $\Sigma$ stretches along the axes. Then $U$ rotates the result into place. The dashed ellipse is the final target. Drag the handles to change $A$. When $\det A \lt 0$ the second stretch passes through zero and flips the flag, which is how the orientation reversal from lesson 02 shows up. Make the columns parallel and $\sigma_2 \to 0$: the ellipse collapses to a segment and the rank drops to 1 (lesson 03).
    Worked example 2: SVD by hand

    $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5\end{bmatrix}$. First $A^\top A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25\end{bmatrix}$.

    Its trace is 50 and its determinant is $625 - 400 = 225$, so $\lambda^2 - 50\lambda + 225 = 0$, giving $\lambda = 45, 5$. Eigenvectors, as in example 1: $\mathbf{v}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$.

    Singular values: $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5 \approx 6.708$ and $\sigma_2 = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236$.

    Left vectors: $A\mathbf{v}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(3, 9)$, so $\mathbf{u}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(3,9)/(3\sqrt5) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(1, 3)$. And $A\mathbf{v}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(3,-1)$, so $\mathbf{u}_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3,-1)$. Check that $\mathbf{u}_1\cdot\mathbf{u}_2 = 3 - 3 = 0$. ✓

    Cross-check with lesson 02: $|\det A| = 15$, and $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 3\sqrt5\cdot\sqrt5 = 15$. ✓ Rotations don't change area, so all the area scaling lives in $\Sigma$.

    Notice that $A$'s eigenvalues are 5 and 3 (it's triangular, so they sit on the diagonal), which are not the singular values. For non-symmetric matrices the two decompositions answer different questions.

    Eigenvalues vs singular values. Eigenvalues answer "which directions stay put, and what happens when I apply $A$ over and over?" (dynamics, power iteration). Singular values answer "how much can $A$ stretch anything, and in which directions?" (size, rank, compression, conditioning). They coincide only for symmetric positive semi-definite matrices. For any symmetric matrix, $\sigma_i = |\lambda_i|$.

    The SVD exposes everything lesson 03 cared about at once. The rank is the number of non-zero $\sigma_i$. The $\mathbf{u}_i$ with $\sigma_i > 0$ span the column space. The $\mathbf{v}_i$ with $\sigma_i = 0$ span the null space. And $\|A\mathbf{x}\| \le \sigma_1\|\mathbf{x}\|$ always, so $\sigma_1$ is the largest stretch, called the spectral norm. The ratio $\sigma_1/\sigma_n$ is the condition number, which measures how badly $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ amplifies errors, and how hard gradient descent struggles on a quadratic (lessons 06 and 18).

    6. Low-rank approximation: keep the big pieces

    The outer-product form $A = \sum_i \sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top$ writes $A$ as a sum of rank-1 "layers", sorted from most to least important. Truncate after $k$ terms:

    $$A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top.$$

    Because $U$ and $V$ are orthogonal, the Frobenius norm (the square root of the sum of all squared entries) ignores the rotations, and the error is exactly what you threw away:

    $$\|A - A_k\|_F = \Big\|\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top\Big\|_F = \sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2 + \sigma_{k+2}^2 + \cdots}.$$

    Eckart–Young theorem. $A_k$ is the best rank-$k$ approximation of $A$: no matrix $B$ of rank $\le k$ has a smaller $\|A - B\|_F$ (or a smaller spectral-norm error, which is $\sigma_{k+1}$). The workbook checks this numerically against random competitors.

    Storage drops from $mn$ numbers to $k(m+n+1)$. If the singular values decay fast, a small $k$ captures almost everything. Natural images, and the weight updates of fine-tuned language models, both behave like this.

    Try it: a 64×64 procedurally drawn image, treated as a matrix of brightness values. Slide $k$ to rebuild it from its top $k$ singular triplets. Big flat shapes (sky gradient, house, ground) appear within a handful of terms, and the round sun by $k \approx 10$. The noisy texture patch in the corner needs nearly all of them, because fine, unstructured detail lives in the small singular values. Notice also the blocky "plaid" artifacts at small $k$: each rank-1 layer $\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top$ is a product of a column pattern and a row pattern. The right panel shows all 64 singular values on a log scale. The ones you keep are blue. The readout compares the measured error with the Eckart–Young prediction from the dropped $\sigma$'s. They always agree.

    LoRA (lesson 25) fine-tunes a huge model by learning a low-rank update $\Delta W = BA$ with $B$ of size $d\times r$ and $A$ of size $r\times d$, $r \approx 8$. That's a bet that the change you need lives in a few directions, which is the Eckart–Young picture. Low-rank structure also shows up in embedding tables, in attention's $QK^\top$ (rank at most the head dimension, lesson 22), and in model compression.

    7. PCA: the directions of greatest variance

    Now point all of this at data. You have $n$ samples in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the rows of $X$. You want the single direction $\mathbf{u}$ (unit length) along which the data is most spread out, because that direction carries the most information.

    First center the data by subtracting the column means, so each row $\mathbf{x}_i$ is now relative to the mean. The coordinate of sample $i$ along $\mathbf{u}$ is the scalar projection $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_i$ (lesson 01). Its variance across the dataset is

    $$\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbf{u}} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{x}_i)^2 = \mathbf{u}^\top\Big(\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top\Big)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^\top C\,\mathbf{u},$$

    where $C = \frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X$ (with $X$ centered) is the covariance matrix. It's symmetric and positive semi-definite. Now apply the spectral theorem: $C = Q\Lambda Q^\top$. Write $\mathbf{u}$ in the eigenbasis, $\mathbf{w} = Q^\top\mathbf{u}$. That's still a unit vector, because rotations preserve length. Then

    $$\mathbf{u}^\top C\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{w}^\top\Lambda\mathbf{w} = \lambda_1 w_1^2 + \lambda_2 w_2^2 + \cdots + \lambda_d w_d^2 \;\le\; \lambda_1(w_1^2 + \cdots + w_d^2) = \lambda_1.$$

    Equality holds when $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{e}_1$, i.e. $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{q}_1$. The direction of maximum variance is the top eigenvector of the covariance, and the variance along it is $\lambda_1$. Repeat the argument among directions orthogonal to $\mathbf{q}_1$ and you get $\mathbf{q}_2$, and so on. These are the principal components. The fraction $\lambda_i/\sum_j\lambda_j$ is the variance explained by component $i$. (The denominator is $\operatorname{tr}C$, the total variance, by section 2's trace fact.)

    Try it: 300 samples from a 2-D Gaussian. The sliders set its spread and tilt. The arrows are the principal axes ($\pm2\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ along each eigenvector of the sample covariance). Drag the pink handle to choose a direction $\mathbf{u}$. The dots on the pink line are the projections, and the right panel plots their variance $\mathbf{u}^\top C\mathbf{u}$ against the angle of $\mathbf{u}$. The curve peaks at exactly PC1 ($\lambda_1$) and bottoms out at PC2 ($\lambda_2$), 90° away. Make the cloud round ($\sigma_1 \approx \sigma_2$) and the curve flattens: no direction is special, and the principal axes start to jitter.

    PCA via the SVD

    You don't need to form $C$ at all. Take the SVD of the centered data, $X = U\Sigma V^\top$. Then

    $$C = \frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X = \frac{1}{n-1}V\Sigma U^\top U\Sigma V^\top = V\,\frac{\Sigma^2}{n-1}\,V^\top.$$

    That's an eigendecomposition of $C$. The principal components are the right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_i$, and the variances are $\sigma_i^2/(n-1)$. This is how real libraries do it, because forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number and loses precision. The two methods agree up to the sign of each component: if $\mathbf{q}$ is an eigenvector, so is $-\mathbf{q}$. The workbook makes you check this.

    Projecting onto the top $k$ components, $Z = XV_k$, gives each sample $k$ coordinates instead of $d$. Mapping back, $\hat X = ZV_k^\top = X V_kV_k^\top$, is exactly the rank-$k$ approximation $X_k$ from section 6. So PCA is Eckart–Young applied to centered data.

    Worked example 3: PCA on five points

    Data: $(-2,-1), (-1,-1), (0,0), (1,1), (2,1)$. The mean is $(0,0)$, so it's already centered.

    Sums: $\sum x^2 = 4+1+0+1+4 = 10$, $\sum xy = 2+1+0+1+2 = 6$, $\sum y^2 = 1+1+0+1+1 = 4$. Divide by $n-1 = 4$: $C = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 & 1.5 \\ 1.5 & 1\end{bmatrix}$.

    Trace $3.5$, determinant $2.5 - 2.25 = 0.25$. So $\lambda = \tfrac{3.5 \pm \sqrt{12.25 - 1}}{2} = \tfrac{3.5 \pm 3.354}{2}$, giving $\lambda_1 \approx 3.427$ and $\lambda_2 \approx 0.073$.

    PC1: the first row of $C - \lambda_1 I$ is $(-0.927, 1.5)$, so $\mathbf{q}_1 \propto (1.5, 0.927)$, which normalizes to $(0.851, 0.526)$. That's a line about 31.7° above the x-axis, right through the cloud.

    Variance explained: $3.427/3.5 \approx 97.9\%$. These 2-D points are essentially 1-D. Store one number per point, $z_i = \mathbf{q}_1\cdot\mathbf{x}_i$, and lose only 2% of the variance.

    Two classic PCA bugs. (1) Forgetting to center: without subtracting the mean, the "top direction" just points at the mean. (2) Mixing units: if one feature is in millimetres and another in kilometres, PCA will declare the millimetre feature "most important" purely because its numbers are bigger. Standardize the features (divide by their standard deviations) when units differ.

    PCA is the first thing to try when you want to look at high-dimensional data. Project 768-d word embeddings onto their top 2 components and clusters appear. It's also used to decorrelate ("whiten") inputs, to compress features, and to analyze what a network has learned: researchers run PCA on hidden activations to find dominant directions. The "variance along $\mathbf{u}$ is $\mathbf{u}^\top C\mathbf{u}$" identity reappears in initialization (lesson 18) and in the Gaussian chapter (lesson 09).

    8. Problems

    Pencil and paper first. Check numbers with the boxes, then read the solutions.

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}$. Enter the eigenvalues, largest first:

    Solution

    $\operatorname{tr}A = 7$ and $\det A = 10$, so $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2) = 0$. For $\lambda=5$: $A - 5I$ has first row $(-1, 1)$, so $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1,1)$. Check: $A(1,1) = (5,5)$. ✓ For $\lambda = 2$: $A - 2I$ has first row $(2, 1)$, so $\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1, 2)$. Check: $A(-1,2) = (-2, 4)$. ✓ The eigenvectors are not orthogonal: $(1,1)\cdot(-1,2) = 1$, because $A$ isn't symmetric.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    Without solving any quadratic, give the sum and the product of the eigenvalues of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{bmatrix}$ (in that order). What does the sign of the product tell you?

    Solution

    Sum = trace = $5$, product = determinant = $4 - 6 = -2$. A negative product means the eigenvalues are real with opposite signs ($\approx 5.372$ and $-0.372$). Along one eigen-line the matrix stretches, along the other it flips. That's why $\det \lt 0$: orientation is reversed (lesson 02).

    Problem 3
    core · proof

    Let $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$. Show that (a) $A^k\mathbf{v} = \lambda^k\mathbf{v}$; (b) if $A$ is invertible, $A^{-1}\mathbf{v} = \lambda^{-1}\mathbf{v}$ (and why $\lambda \ne 0$); (c) $(A + cI)\mathbf{v} = (\lambda + c)\mathbf{v}$. Then explain how (c) lets power iteration find the smallest eigenvalue of a symmetric positive definite matrix.

    Hint

    For (b), multiply both sides of $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ by $A^{-1}$. For the last part, which eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$ is the biggest?

    Solution

    (a) Induction: $A^{k}\mathbf{v} = A(A^{k-1}\mathbf{v}) = A(\lambda^{k-1}\mathbf{v}) = \lambda^{k-1}A\mathbf{v} = \lambda^k\mathbf{v}$. (b) If $\lambda = 0$ then $A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ with $\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}$, so $A$ has a non-trivial null space and isn't invertible. Otherwise $\mathbf{v} = A^{-1}A\mathbf{v} = \lambda A^{-1}\mathbf{v}$, so divide by $\lambda$. (c) $(A + cI)\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} + c\mathbf{v}$.

    Smallest eigenvalue: the eigenvalues of $A^{-1}$ are $1/\lambda_i$, and the largest of these comes from the smallest $\lambda_i$. So run power iteration on $A^{-1}$ (in practice, solve $A\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}_k$ each step instead of inverting). This is inverse iteration. With a shift, $(A - cI)^{-1}$ finds the eigenvalue closest to $c$.

    Problem 4
    core

    A matrix has eigenvalues $5$ and $2$. Power iteration's error shrinks by $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|$ per step. Starting from an error of 1, what is the smallest number of steps that brings the error below $10^{-6}$?

    Solution

    We need $0.4^k \lt 10^{-6}$, i.e. $k > \ln(10^{-6})/\ln(0.4) = 15.08$. So $k = 16$. If instead the eigenvalues were 5 and 4.9 (ratio 0.98), you'd need $k > 684$. A tiny spectral gap makes power iteration crawl.

    Problem 5
    core

    Find the singular values of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ -1 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ (largest first). Then compute its eigenvalues. What do you notice?

    Hint

    Form $A^\top A$, find its eigenvalues, take square roots.

    Solution

    $A^\top A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 5\end{bmatrix}$, with trace 10 and determinant 16, so $\lambda = 8, 2$. That gives $\sigma_1 = 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.828$ and $\sigma_2 = \sqrt2 \approx 1.414$. Check: $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 4 = |\det A|$. ✓

    Eigenvalues of $A$: trace 3, determinant 4, so the discriminant is $9 - 16 \lt 0$ and the eigenvalues are the complex pair $1.5 \pm 1.323i$. $A$ rotates every direction, so it has no real eigenvectors. But it still has a perfectly real SVD. The SVD always exists; real eigenvectors don't.

    Problem 6
    core · proof

    (a) Show that for any matrix $A$, the eigenvalues of $A^\top A$ are $\ge 0$. (b) Show that a covariance matrix $C = \frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X$ satisfies $\mathbf{u}^\top C\mathbf{u} \ge 0$ for every $\mathbf{u}$, and interpret this in words.

    Solution

    (a) If $A^\top A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ with $\|\mathbf{v}\| = 1$, then $\lambda = \mathbf{v}^\top A^\top A\mathbf{v} = \|A\mathbf{v}\|^2 \ge 0$. (b) $\mathbf{u}^\top C\mathbf{u} = \frac{1}{n-1}\|X\mathbf{u}\|^2 \ge 0$. In words, this is the variance of the data projected onto $\mathbf{u}$, and a variance can't be negative. Matrices with this property are called positive semi-definite. They're exactly the symmetric matrices whose eigenvalues are all $\ge 0$. You'll meet them again as Hessians at a minimum (lesson 06).

    Problem 7
    core

    A matrix has singular values $10, 5, 2, 1$. What is the Frobenius error $\|A - A_2\|_F$ of its best rank-2 approximation? What fraction of $\|A\|_F^2$ (the "energy") does $A_2$ keep?

    Solution

    By Eckart–Young, $\|A - A_2\|_F = \sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236$. Energy: $\|A\|_F^2 = \sum\sigma_i^2 = 100+25+4+1 = 130$, and $A_2$ keeps $125/130 \approx 96.2\%$.

    Problem 8
    core

    A centered 2-D dataset has covariance $C = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2\end{bmatrix}$. Find the first principal component and the fraction of variance it explains.

    Solution

    $\operatorname{tr}C = 7$ and $\det C = 6$, so $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = 0$, giving $\lambda = 6, 1$. $C - 6I$ has first row $(-1, 2)$, so $\mathbf{q}_1 = (2,1)/\sqrt5$. Variance explained $= 6/7 \approx 0.857$. The second component is $(-1,2)/\sqrt5$ with variance 1.

    Problem 9
    stretch

    A $512\times512$ grayscale image is stored as its rank-20 SVD truncation, keeping $\mathbf{u}_i$, $\mathbf{v}_i$ and $\sigma_i$ for $i \le 20$. How many numbers is that? Above what rank $k$ does the truncation stop saving space?

    Solution

    $k(m + n + 1) = 20 \cdot 1025 = 20{,}500$ numbers, versus $512^2 = 262{,}144$: about 7.8%. Break-even is $k(1025) = 262{,}144$, i.e. $k \approx 255.75$. So any $k \ge 256$ saves nothing, roughly half the full rank. Low-rank only pays when the spectrum decays fast.

    Problem 10
    stretch · proof

    Let $A$ be symmetric with eigen-decomposition $Q\Lambda Q^\top$. Show that its singular values are $|\lambda_i|$, and find them for $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}$ (largest first).

    Hint

    Compute $A^\top A$ using $Q^\top Q = I$.

    Solution

    $A^\top A = A^2 = Q\Lambda Q^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top = Q\Lambda^2Q^\top$, so the eigenvalues of $A^\top A$ are $\lambda_i^2$ and $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i^2} = |\lambda_i|$. Explicitly: $A = Q\,|\Lambda|\,(Q S)^\top$, where $S$ is the diagonal matrix of signs of the $\lambda_i$ (absorbing each sign into a right singular vector). For $\begin{bmatrix} 1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$: trace 2, determinant $-3$, so $\lambda = 3, -1$ and $\sigma = 3, 1$.

    9. Quick check

    How many real eigen-directions does a rotation of the plane by 90° have?
    An eigenvector must stay on its own span. Rotation by 90° moves every direction. Algebraically $\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$ has only complex roots $\pm i$. (In 3-D, the rotation axis would be an eigenvector with $\lambda = 1$, but the plane has no axis.)
    Power iteration is converging painfully slowly. The most likely reason:
    The error shrinks like $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k$. A large $\lambda_1$ alone doesn't matter, since normalization removes the overall scale. A ratio near 1 does.
    You run PCA on (height in mm, weight in kg) without standardizing. PC1 points almost exactly along the height axis. What's the best explanation?
    Variance scales with the square of the units. Millimetres give variances in the thousands, while kilograms give tens. PCA maximizes raw variance, so it's not unit-invariant. Standardize first.
    A $3\times3$ matrix has singular values $5, 3, 0$. Which is true?
    Rank = number of non-zero singular values. The $\mathbf{v}_3$ with $\sigma_3 = 0$ spans the null space, and the image is a 2-D ellipse inside 3-D space. $|\det A| = \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = 0$. The eigenvalues needn't match the singular values unless $A$ is symmetric.
