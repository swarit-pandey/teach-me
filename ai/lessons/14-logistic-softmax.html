Logistic & softmax classification

Regression predicts a number. Classification predicts a category: spam or not, which digit, which next token. The recipe barely changes. Compute a linear score, squash it into a probability, and train by maximum likelihood. The result is the most important loss in deep learning, the cross-entropy, and a gradient with an almost suspiciously simple form: (prediction − truth) × input.

By the end you can

  • Explain the sigmoid, logits and log-odds, and read a 2-D logistic model as a weight vector perpendicular to its decision boundary.
  • Derive the log-loss from the Bernoulli likelihood, and compute it stably from logits.
  • Derive $\partial\ell/\partial z = p - y$, and so $\nabla_{\mathbf w} = (p-y)\mathbf x$, and train logistic regression with gradient descent.
  • Generalize to $K$ classes with softmax and cross-entropy, with gradient $P - Y$.
  • Say what a linear classifier can't do, and how feature maps (and, next, neural networks) fix it.

    1. From scores to probabilities: the sigmoid

    Labels are now $y \in \{0, 1\}$. Why not just run linear regression on them? Because $\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x + b$ ranges over all of $\mathbb R$: it predicts 1.7 or −0.4, which aren't probabilities. And a few points far from the boundary drag the least-squares line around even though they're already classified correctly. We want a model that outputs $P(y=1\mid\mathbf x) \in (0,1)$.

    Keep the linear score $z = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x + b$ (called the logit), and squash it with the sigmoid (logistic) function:

    $$p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}.$$

    It maps $-\infty \to 0$, $0 \to \tfrac12$ and $+\infty \to 1$, smoothly. Three identities do most of the work in this lesson (problem 3 asks you to prove them):

    $$\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z), \qquad \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\,\big(1-\sigma(z)\big), \qquad z = \log\frac{p}{1-p}.$$

    The last one says what the score means. $z$ is the log-odds. A logit of 0 means even odds, $+2$ means odds of $e^2 \approx 7.4$ to 1, and adding 1 to $z$ multiplies the odds by $e$. So logistic regression is a linear model of the log-odds. Each weight $w_j$ says how much one unit of feature $j$ shifts the log-odds.

    Try it: 1-D data, with class 1 drawn at the top and class 0 at the bottom. Adjust $w$ and $b$ to fit the curve to them. The bars under the axis show each point's loss, $-\log(\text{probability given to its true class})$, which section 3 derives. Points far on the wrong side of the threshold $x = -b/w$ produce huge bars. Increase $w$: the curve gets steeper and more confident. That helps the correctly classified points and badly hurts the misclassified ones.

    2. Geometry: the decision boundary

    To classify, predict 1 when $p \gt \tfrac12$, which is when $z \gt 0$. The decision boundary is the set $\{\mathbf x : \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x + b = 0\}$: a line in 2-D, a plane in 3-D, a hyperplane in general. From lesson 01:

    • $\mathbf w$ is perpendicular to the boundary. Moving along the boundary doesn't change $\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x$, so the score can't change either.
    • $z/\|\mathbf w\|$ is the signed distance from $\mathbf x$ to the boundary (a scalar projection onto $\hat{\mathbf w}$, shifted by $b$). Points far on the positive side get confident probabilities near 1.
    • Scaling $(\mathbf w, b)$ by $c \gt 0$ leaves the boundary unchanged but multiplies every logit by $c$. A longer $\mathbf w$ means a steeper, more confident sigmoid across the boundary.
    Try it: drag the tip of the weight vector $\mathbf w$ (and slide $b$). The background shows $p = \sigma(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x + b)$, blue for class 1 and pink for class 0. The solid line is the boundary $p = \tfrac12$, always perpendicular to $\mathbf w$. The dashed lines are $z = \pm1$ ($p \approx 0.73$ and $0.27$). Make $\mathbf w$ longer without turning it: the boundary stays put, the dashed band narrows, and the loss changes.

    3. The loss: maximum likelihood gives log-loss

    We don't invent a loss. We derive one, as in lesson 10. The model says $y\mid\mathbf x$ is a Bernoulli coin with heads-probability $p = \sigma(z)$. Its probability mass function, in one line, is

    $$P(y\mid\mathbf x) = p^{\,y}(1-p)^{1-y} \qquad (\text{this equals } p \text{ if } y = 1, \text{ and } 1-p \text{ if } y = 0).$$

    With independent examples, the likelihood is a product. Its negative log, averaged, is the log-loss or binary cross-entropy:

    $$L(\mathbf w, b) = -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \Big[y_i\log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big].$$

    This is exactly lesson 11's cross-entropy $H(q, p)$ between the true label distribution $q$ (all mass on $y_i$) and the model's $p$. Each term is $-\log(\text{probability assigned to the true class})$. A correct answer at 99% costs $0.01$, a coin flip costs $\log 2 \approx 0.69$, and a confident wrong answer at 1% costs $4.6$. The loss grows without bound as the model becomes certain of the wrong class.

    Compute it from logits

    If you compute p = sigmoid(z) and then log(p), a logit of $-800$ makes $p$ round to exactly 0, and the loss becomes inf. Substitute $\log\sigma(z) = -\log(1+e^{-z})$ and $\log(1-\sigma(z)) = \log\sigma(-z) = -\log(1+e^{z})$ instead, and simplify:

    $$\ell(z, y) = y\log(1+e^{-z}) + (1-y)\log(1+e^{z}) = \log(1 + e^{z}) - y\,z.$$

    (Use $\log(1+e^{-z}) = \log(1+e^{z}) - z$.) Here $\log(1+e^z)$ is the softplus, computed stably by np.logaddexp(0, z). Every framework's BCEWithLogits / binary_cross_entropy_with_logits is this formula. That's why models output logits and leave the sigmoid to the loss.

    Try it: the blue curve is the log-loss for a positive example ($y=1$) as a function of its logit $z$, and the pink curve is the same for a negative example. Grey dashed is the squared error $(\sigma(z) - 1)^2$, the "obvious" alternative. Slide $z$ far to the left (confidently wrong). The log-loss slope stays near −1, a strong corrective push. The squared error goes flat: its gradient vanishes exactly when the model is most wrong.

    4. The gradient: (p − y) x

    Differentiate the logit form, $\ell = \log(1+e^z) - yz$, with respect to $z$:

    $$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z} = \frac{e^z}{1+e^z} - y = \sigma(z) - y = p - y.$$

    That's the whole derivation. The error signal is "predicted probability minus truth". Then the chain rule through $z = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x + b$ (where $\partial z/\partial\mathbf w = \mathbf x$ and $\partial z/\partial b = 1$) gives, averaged over the data,

    $$\boxed{\;\nabla_{\mathbf w}L = \frac1n\sum_i (p_i - y_i)\,\mathbf x_i = \frac1n X^\top(\mathbf p - \mathbf y),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac1n\sum_i (p_i - y_i)\;}$$

    Compare linear regression (lesson 13): $\frac2n X^\top(\hat{\mathbf y} - \mathbf y)$. It's the same shape, error × input. This is no coincidence. Both are generalized linear models trained with their natural likelihood, and that pairing always gives this form. Problem 9 shows why sigmoid + squared error does not.

    Worked example: one gradient step by hand

    One example $\mathbf x = (1, 2)$ with $y = 1$. Start at $\mathbf w = (0,0)$, $b = 0$, learning rate $\eta = 1$.

    Forward: $z = 0$, so $p = \sigma(0) = 0.5$ and $\ell = \log(1 + e^0) - 1\cdot0 = \log 2 \approx 0.693$.

    Backward: $p - y = -0.5$. So $\nabla_{\mathbf w} = -0.5\,(1, 2) = (-0.5, -1)$ and $\partial_b = -0.5$.

    Update: $\mathbf w = (0.5, 1)$ and $b = 0.5$. Now $z = 0.5 + 2 + 0.5 = 3$, $p = \sigma(3) \approx 0.953$, and $\ell = \log(1 + e^{-3}) \approx 0.049$. The step moved $\mathbf w$ toward $\mathbf x$ (a positive example pulls $\mathbf w$ toward itself; a negative one pushes it away). Lesson 01's "dot product = alignment" is doing the classifying.

    Convexity. Differentiating again gives the Hessian $H = \frac1n X^\top S X$ with $S = \operatorname{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))$. It's positive semi-definite, since $\mathbf v^\top H\mathbf v = \frac1n\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)(\mathbf x_i\cdot\mathbf v)^2 \ge 0$. So the log-loss is convex, and gradient descent can't get stuck in a bad local minimum. Unlike lesson 13, though, $\nabla L = 0$ is now a non-linear equation in $\mathbf w$ (the $p_i$ depend on it). There is no closed form, so we iterate.

    Separable data has no minimizer. If a line separates the classes perfectly, scaling $\mathbf w$ up pushes every $p_i$ toward its label and the loss toward 0. It never reaches 0, though, and $\|\mathbf w\| \to \infty$ as GD runs. In the live demo below, watch the boundary sharpen forever on separable blobs. The fix is to add a penalty $\frac\lambda2\|\mathbf w\|^2$ (L2 regularization, lesson 15). It gives a finite, well-behaved answer.

    5. K classes: softmax and cross-entropy

    For $K$ classes, give each class its own weight vector and compute $K$ logits: $z_k = \mathbf w_k\cdot\mathbf x + b_k$, or $\mathbf z = W^\top\mathbf x + \mathbf b$ with $W \in \mathbb R^{d\times K}$. Turn them into a probability distribution with the softmax (stable version: lesson 12):

    $$P(y = k\mid\mathbf x) = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf z)_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}}.$$

    The outputs are positive and sum to 1. Adding the same constant to every logit changes nothing, so only differences between logits matter. For $K = 2$, $\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf z)_1 = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(z_1 - z_0)}} = \sigma(z_1 - z_0)$: logistic regression is the two-class special case (problem 7).

    The likelihood is now a categorical distribution, and the negative log-likelihood is the cross-entropy:

    $$\ell(\mathbf z, y) = -\log\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf z)_y = \underbrace{\log\sum_j e^{z_j}}_{\text{logsumexp}} - z_y.$$

    Its gradient follows the same pattern. Writing $\mathbf e_y$ for the one-hot vector of the label,

    $$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z_k} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf z)_k - [k = y] \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \nabla_{\mathbf z}\ell = \mathbf p - \mathbf e_y,$$

    because $\partial\,\text{logsumexp}/\partial z_k = e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j} = p_k$. Batched over the data matrix, this is $\nabla_W L = \frac1n X^\top(P - Y)$, with $Y$ the one-hot label matrix. The update raises the true class's logit by $1 - p_y$ and lowers every other logit in proportion to how much probability it stole.

    Try it: set three logits and choose the true class. Left: the probabilities. Right: the gradient $\mathbf p - \mathbf e_y$ that training would push back into the logits. The true class always gets a negative entry (its logit goes up), the others positive, and the entries always sum to zero. Lower the temperature $T$ (logits divided by $T$) to sharpen the softmax toward argmax. You'll meet $T$ again in sampling from language models (lesson 25).

    A GPT's final layer is exactly this: softmax regression over a vocabulary of ~50,000 tokens, applied to the feature vector the transformer computed. "Training a language model" means minimizing this cross-entropy for the next token, averaged over billions of positions (lesson 24). The gradient that starts backpropagation is $\mathbf p - \mathbf e_y$.

    Geometrically, the predicted class is $\arg\max_k z_k$. Between classes $j$ and $k$ the boundary is $z_j = z_k$, i.e. $(\mathbf w_j - \mathbf w_k)\cdot\mathbf x + (b_j - b_k) = 0$, another hyperplane. The decision regions are convex polygons (intersections of half-spaces). That's both the strength and the limit of linear classifiers.

    6. Training live, and the limits of linear

    Here's the whole thing running in your browser: softmax regression trained by full-batch gradient descent, $\mathbf W \leftarrow \mathbf W - \eta\,\frac1n\Phi^\top(P - Y)$, where $\Phi$ holds the (standardized) features. The background is the decision region of the current model. Its color is the predicted class, and its intensity is the model's confidence.

    Try it: press play on the blobs and watch the boundary settle. On separable two-blob data, keep it running: the weights keep growing and the boundary keeps sharpening, which is the warning box above in action. Now pick XOR or circles with linear features: no straight line works, and accuracy stalls near chance. Switch to quadratic features and the same algorithm bends its boundary. Try spirals: even cubic features struggle. Hand-designing features doesn't scale. Lesson 16's neural networks learn the feature map $\phi$ instead.

    "Quadratic features" means running the linear classifier on $\phi(\mathbf x) = (x_1, x_2, x_1^2, x_2^2, x_1x_2)$. The model is linear in $\phi$, so the gradient, convexity and code are unchanged, but its boundary in the original $(x_1,x_2)$ plane is a conic: a circle, an ellipse or a hyperbola. This is lesson 13's polynomial trick applied to classification.

    7. Evaluating a classifier

    Accuracy is the fraction predicted correctly. It can mislead. With 99% negatives, predicting "negative" always scores 99%. The confusion matrix $C_{ij}$ counts examples of true class $i$ predicted as $j$, so the diagonal holds the correct predictions and everything off it is a specific mistake. From it, per class:

    $$\text{precision}_k = \frac{C_{kk}}{\sum_i C_{ik}}\;\;(\text{of predicted } k\text{, how many were } k), \qquad \text{recall}_k = \frac{C_{kk}}{\sum_j C_{kj}}\;\;(\text{of true } k\text{, how many we found}).$$
    Worked example: a 3-class confusion matrix

    Rows are true classes, columns predictions: $C = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 & 0\\ 2 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 8\end{bmatrix}$, with $n = 20$.

    Accuracy $= (5+3+8)/20 = 0.8$. Class 0: precision $= 5/(5+2+0) = 0.714$, recall $= 5/(5+1+0) = 0.833$. Class 1: precision $3/5 = 0.6$, recall $3/5 = 0.6$. Class 2: precision $8/8 = 1$, recall $8/9 = 0.889$. The matrix also shows where the errors are: classes 0 and 1 get confused with each other, and class 2 is well separated.

    Remember too that log-loss and accuracy measure different things. Accuracy only looks at the argmax. Log-loss rewards calibrated probabilities. Two models with equal accuracy can differ a lot in log-loss, and you train on log-loss because it's smooth and accuracy isn't.

    8. Problems

    Problem 1
    warm-up

    A logistic model outputs $p = 0.8$ for an email being spam. What is the logit $z$?

    Solution

    $z = \log\frac{p}{1-p} = \log\frac{0.8}{0.2} = \log 4 \approx 1.386$. Check: $\sigma(\log 4) = 1/(1 + 1/4) = 0.8$ ✓.

    Problem 2
    warm-up

    An example has logit $z = 2$ but true label $y = 0$. What is its log-loss?

    Solution

    $\ell = \log(1 + e^{2}) - 0\cdot 2 = \log(8.389) \approx 2.127$. Equivalently, $p = \sigma(2) \approx 0.881$ and $-\log(1 - 0.881) \approx 2.127$.

    Problem 3
    core · proof

    Prove (a) $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$, (b) $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$, and (c) that $\sigma^{-1}(p) = \log\frac{p}{1-p}$.

    Solution

    (a) $1 - \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} = \frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} = \frac{1}{e^{z} + 1} = \sigma(-z)$, multiplying top and bottom by $e^z$.

    (b) $\sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2} = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} = \sigma(z)\,\sigma(-z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$. The maximum is $1/4$, at $z=0$.

    (c) $p = 1/(1 + e^{-z}) \Rightarrow e^{-z} = 1/p - 1 = (1-p)/p \Rightarrow z = \log\frac{p}{1-p}$.

    Problem 4
    core · derivation

    Derive $\partial\ell/\partial z = \sigma(z) - y$ directly from $\ell = -[y\log\sigma(z) + (1-y)\log(1-\sigma(z))]$ using problem 3(b), without first simplifying to the logit form.

    Solution

    $\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\log\sigma = \frac{\sigma(1-\sigma)}{\sigma} = 1-\sigma$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\log(1-\sigma) = \frac{-\sigma(1-\sigma)}{1-\sigma} = -\sigma$. So $\partial\ell/\partial z = -[y(1-\sigma) - (1-y)\sigma] = -[y - y\sigma - \sigma + y\sigma] = \sigma - y$. The $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ from the chain rule cancels exactly against the $1/p$ from the log. That cancellation is why log-loss and sigmoid belong together.

    Problem 5
    core

    Two examples: $\mathbf x_1 = (1, 2)$ with $y_1 = 1$, and $\mathbf x_2 = (-1, 0)$ with $y_2 = 0$. Starting at $\mathbf w = (0,0)$, $b = 0$, take one batch gradient-descent step with $\eta = 1$ on the mean log-loss. Enter $w_1, w_2, b$.

    Solution

    Both $p_i = 0.5$, so $\mathbf p - \mathbf y = (-0.5, 0.5)$. $\nabla_{\mathbf w} = \frac12[(-0.5)(1,2) + (0.5)(-1,0)] = \frac12(-1, -1) = (-0.5, -0.5)$, and $\partial_b = \frac12(-0.5 + 0.5) = 0$. Step: $\mathbf w = (0.5, 0.5)$, $b = 0$.

    Problem 6
    core

    Logits $\mathbf z = (2, 1, 0)$ for three classes. Compute the softmax probabilities, then the cross-entropy if the true class is the third one ($y = 2$).

    Solution

    $e^{\mathbf z} = (7.389, 2.718, 1)$, summing to $11.107$. So $\mathbf p \approx (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$. Cross-entropy is $-\log 0.090 \approx 2.408$. Via logsumexp: $\log 11.107 - 0 = 2.408$ ✓. If the true class were the first, the loss would be only $0.408$.

    Problem 7
    core · proof

    Show that softmax regression with $K=2$ is equivalent to logistic regression. What do the two weight vectors $\mathbf w_0, \mathbf w_1$ map to? Why does this mean softmax parameters aren't unique?

    Solution

    $\operatorname{softmax}(z_0, z_1)_1 = \frac{e^{z_1}}{e^{z_0} + e^{z_1}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(z_1 - z_0)}} = \sigma(z_1 - z_0)$. So it's logistic regression with $\mathbf w = \mathbf w_1 - \mathbf w_0$ and $b = b_1 - b_0$. Only the difference matters: adding any vector $\mathbf c$ to every $\mathbf w_k$ leaves all predictions unchanged. The parameterization has a redundant direction, which L2 regularization (lesson 15) pins down.

    Problem 8
    core · conceptual

    The training data are linearly separable by $(\mathbf w^*, b^*)$. Show that $L(c\,\mathbf w^*, c\,b^*) \to 0$ as $c \to \infty$, and conclude that the unregularized log-loss has no minimizer. What does gradient descent do?

    Solution

    Separable means $y_i = 1 \Rightarrow z_i^* \gt 0$ and $y_i = 0 \Rightarrow z_i^* \lt 0$. Scaling by $c$ sends positive logits to $+\infty$ and negative ones to $-\infty$, so every $\ell_i \to 0$. Since $\ell_i \gt 0$ for every finite logit, the infimum 0 is never attained, and there's no minimizer. GD keeps decreasing the loss by growing $\|\mathbf w\|$ indefinitely (its direction converges to the max-margin separator, a deep result). In practice, add $\frac\lambda2\|\mathbf w\|^2$ or stop early (lesson 15).

    Problem 9
    stretch

    Suppose you trained with squared error on probabilities, $\ell_{\text{sq}} = (\sigma(z) - y)^2$. Compute $\partial\ell_{\text{sq}}/\partial z$, and evaluate it and the log-loss gradient at $z = -5$, $y = 1$ (a confidently wrong prediction). Enter the squared-error gradient.

    Solution

    $\partial\ell_{\text{sq}}/\partial z = 2(\sigma - y)\,\sigma(1-\sigma)$. At $z=-5$: $\sigma \approx 0.00669$, so the gradient is $2(-0.99331)(0.00669)(0.99331) \approx -0.0132$. The log-loss gradient is $\sigma - 1 \approx -0.993$, 75× larger. The extra $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ factor kills the learning signal exactly when it's needed most. It also makes $\ell_{\text{sq}}$ non-convex in $z$. This "saturation" problem comes back with sigmoid activations inside deep networks (lesson 18).

    9. Quick check

    You double both $\mathbf w$ and $b$ of a trained logistic model. What happens?
    The boundary $\{\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x + b = 0\}$ is the same set after scaling. Each logit doubles, so the sigmoid is steeper. Accuracy is unchanged, but the log-loss changes: it goes down for correct points and up for wrong ones.
    Why compute the loss from logits with logaddexp instead of -log(sigmoid(z))?
    Mathematically it's the same function. Numerically, $\log(1+e^{z}) - yz$ stays finite and accurate for any $z$. The loss is convex either way.
    For softmax + cross-entropy, the gradient with respect to the logits is…
    (b) is the gradient with respect to the probabilities, before going back through the softmax. (d) has the sign flipped. With $\mathbf p - \mathbf e_y$, descent raises the true logit (its entry is negative) and lowers the others.
    Linear logistic regression on XOR-shaped data gets ~50% accuracy. What's the most direct fix?
    No line separates XOR, so no amount of optimization helps a linear model in the raw features. With the product feature $x_1x_2$ the classes become linearly separable.
